## Seventeenth-Century Philosophy: Rationalism, Empiricism, and Political Thought (c. 1600–1720)

### Hugo Grotius ☆ (1583–1645)

**Position:** Early Modern Natural Law Theory · Political Philosophy · Philosophy of Law · Law of Nations

* **Name:** Hugo Grotius (Dutch: Hugo de Groot; Latin: *Hugo Grotius*; /ˈɡroʊʃiəs/).
* **Life:** Born in Delft, Dutch Republic; Dutch jurist, philosopher, diplomat, and political thinker. Died in Rostock.
* **Contributions:** One of the principal architects of early modern natural law theory. Developed influential accounts of rights, obligations, property, political authority, war, and the legal relations between political communities.
* **Major Works:** *The Free Sea (Mare Liberum)*, *On the Law of War and Peace (De Iure Belli ac Pacis)*, *The Law of Prize and Booty (De Iure Praedae)*.

**Natural Law and Right Reason**
* Natural Law (*Ius Naturale*), Right Reason, Moral Obligation, Human Sociability.
* Can fundamental moral obligations be discovered through reason independently of political institutions?
* *On the Law of War and Peace*, Prolegomena.

**Rights, Property, and Justice**
* Natural Rights, Property, Ownership, Restitution, Contractual Obligation.
* What makes an individual entitled to possess or transfer property? How do rights generate corresponding obligations?

**The Law of Nations**
* *Ius Gentium*, State Sovereignty, International Obligations, Treaty Law.
* Can independent political communities be bound by common legal principles without a superior political authority?

**Just War Theory**
* Just Cause, Legitimate Authority, Necessity, Proportionality, Conduct in War.
* Under what circumstances may a political community legitimately resort to war?
* *On the Law of War and Peace*.

**Freedom of the Seas**
* Common Use, Maritime Freedom, Property and Jurisdiction.
* Can the sea legitimately become the exclusive property of a political community?
* *The Free Sea*.

**Natural Law and Divine Authority**
* Moral Necessity, Divine Will, the Independence of Natural Law.
* Would natural law retain its normative force independently of divine commands?

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Francisco de Vitoria, Thomas Hobbes, Samuel Pufendorf, John Locke, Natural Rights Theory, International Legal Philosophy.

### Thomas Hobbes ☆ (1588–1679)

**Position:** Early Modern Philosophy · Materialism · Mechanistic Philosophy · Social Contract Theory · Political Absolutism

* **Name:** Thomas Hobbes (/hɒbz/).
* **Life:** Born in Westport, Wiltshire, England; philosopher, political theorist, and translator. His intellectual career was profoundly shaped by the English Civil Wars. Died at Hardwick Hall, Derbyshire.
* **Contributions:** Developed an integrated philosophical system grounded in materialism, mechanistic explanation, human psychology, and political theory. One of the foundational figures of modern social contract philosophy.
* **Major Works:** *The Elements of Law*, *On the Citizen (De Cive)*, *Leviathan*, *On Body (De Corpore)*, *On Man (De Homine)*, *Behemoth*.

**Materialism and Metaphysics**
* Materialism, Body, Motion, Corporeal Substance, Mechanistic Explanation.
* Can all existing things, including human minds, be understood in material terms?
* *De Corpore*, *Leviathan* I.

**Human Nature and Moral Psychology**
* Appetite, Aversion, Desire, Fear, Self-Preservation, Deliberation.
* How do desire and fear explain human behavior? Can practical reasoning be understood mechanistically?
* *Leviathan* I.

**The State of Nature**
* State of Nature, Equality, Competition, Diffidence, Glory, Insecurity.
* Why does the absence of common political authority produce conflict? What makes rational individuals seek peace?
* *Leviathan* XIII.

**Natural Right and Laws of Nature**
* Right of Nature (*Ius Naturale*), Laws of Nature (*Leges Naturales*), Self-Preservation, Rational Obligation.
* What distinguishes a natural liberty from a rational obligation?
* *Leviathan* XIV–XV.

**Social Contract and Political Authority**
* Social Contract, Authorization, Representation, Sovereignty.
* How can political authority arise from agreements between individuals? Why should individuals authorize a common sovereign?
* *Leviathan* XVII–XVIII.

**Sovereignty and Political Obligation**
* Absolute Sovereignty, Political Unity, Law, Coercion, Civil Peace.
* Why does Hobbes argue that sovereign authority must remain undivided? What limits, if any, constrain political obedience?

**Freedom, Determinism, and Responsibility**
* Necessitarianism, Voluntary Action, Liberty, Causation.
* Is freedom compatible with causal necessity?
* *Leviathan* XXI; *Of Liberty and Necessity*.

**Language, Reason, and Scientific Method**
* Names, Definitions, Calculation, Demonstration, Scientific Explanation.
* Why does Hobbes understand reasoning partly as a form of calculation?
* *De Corpore*, *Leviathan* IV–V.

**Religion and Political Authority**
* Religious Authority, Scriptural Interpretation, Civil Sovereignty.
* Why does political peace require regulation of competing claims to religious authority?
* *Leviathan* III–IV.

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Machiavelli, Grotius, Pufendorf, Locke, Rousseau, Modern Contractarianism, Political Realism.

### Pierre Gassendi ☆ (1592–1655)

**Position:** Early Modern Empiricism · Neo-Epicureanism · Atomism · Experimental Natural Philosophy

* **Name:** Pierre Gassendi (Latin: *Petrus Gassendus*).
* **Life:** Born in Champtercier, France; philosopher, astronomer, scientist, and Catholic priest. Died in Paris.
* **Contributions:** Revived and transformed Epicurean atomism, developed an influential empiricist approach to knowledge, and offered major criticisms of Cartesian metaphysics.
* **Major Works:** *Exercitationes Paradoxicae Adversus Aristoteleos*, *Fifth Objections* to Descartes' *Meditations*, *Animadversiones*, *Syntagma Philosophicum* (posthumous).

**Empiricism and the Sources of Knowledge**
* Sensation, Experience, Induction, Fallibility, Empirical Justification.
* Can knowledge of the external world be established independently of sensory experience?
* *Syntagma Philosophicum*.

**Atomism and the Mechanical Philosophy**
* Atoms, Void, Motion, Corpuscular Explanation.
* Can material phenomena be explained through the motion and arrangement of fundamental particles?

**Criticism of Cartesian Rationalism**
* Innate Ideas, Clear and Distinct Perception, the Cogito, Substance Dualism.
* Can intellectual certainty be established through purely rational reflection?
* *Fifth Objections*.

**Scientific Method and Probable Knowledge**
* Experimental Evidence, Hypothesis, Probability, Scientific Explanation.
* How can scientific inquiry produce warranted beliefs without guaranteeing certainty?

**Epicureanism and Christian Philosophy**
* Ancient Atomism, Creation, Immortal Soul, Ethical Pleasure.
* How can Epicurean natural philosophy be revised to accommodate Christian philosophical commitments?

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Epicurus, Descartes, Robert Boyle, Locke, Newton, Early Modern Empiricism.

### René Descartes ★ (1596–1650)

**Position:** Early Modern Philosophy · Cartesianism · Rationalism · Substance Dualism · Mechanistic Natural Philosophy

* **Name:** René Descartes (Latin: *Renatus Cartesius*; /deɪˈkɑːrt/).
* **Life:** Born in La Haye en Touraine, Kingdom of France; philosopher, mathematician, and natural scientist. Lived for many years in the Dutch Republic and died in Stockholm, Sweden.
* **Contributions:** A foundational figure of modern philosophy. Developed a program of methodological doubt, theories of knowledge and rational justification, a dualistic account of mind and body, mechanistic natural philosophy, and analytic geometry.
* **Major Works:** *Rules for the Direction of the Mind*, *Discourse on the Method*, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, *Principles of Philosophy*, *The Passions of the Soul*, *The World*, *Treatise on Man*, *Objections and Replies*.
#### Philosophy of René Descartes

**Philosophical Method and Rational Inquiry**: `foundationalism`
* **Central thesis:** All genuine knowledge forms a single, unified body of "human wisdom." It can be acquired with the certainty of mathematics if the mind follows a correct method. That method has three parts:
  * accept only what is intuited with complete evidence;
  * break complex problems into their simplest elements;
  * proceed in an unbroken order from the simple to the complex.
  
  Descartes's metaphysics can be read as the attempt to give this method an absolutely secure foundation. In the early *Rules* the method itself was simply presupposed.
* **Historical problem:** Descartes was educated at the Jesuit college of La Flèche in a late-scholastic curriculum. It combined Aristotelian logic and natural philosophy with humanist letters. In *Discourse* I he reports two disappointments. Philosophy contained no claim that was not disputed, so it yielded at best probable opinion. Mathematics was certain but had been applied only to the mechanical arts. His collaboration with Isaac Beeckman in 1618–1619 on problems of mathematical physics suggested a new ambition: a general science of order and measure that would extend mathematical certainty across all subject matter. The problem he inherited was thus not primarily skeptical. He sought a reformed method that could replace both the syllogistic dialectic of the schools and the humanist acceptance of mere probability.
* **The unity of knowledge (Rules I):** The sciences are "nothing other than human wisdom (*bona mens*), which always remains one and the same however different the subjects to which it is applied," just as sunlight is not altered by the variety of things it illuminates. This rejects the Aristotelian principle that each science has its own genus and principles and that demonstration cannot cross from one genus to another. For Descartes, method is subject-neutral. What varies is only the material to which a single cognitive power is applied.
* **Intuition and deduction (Rules III):** Two acts of the intellect yield knowledge without fear of error.
  * *Intuition (intuitus):* "the conception of a clear and attentive mind, which is so easy and distinct that there can be no room for doubt about what we are understanding." It arises from the "light of reason" alone. Examples: that one exists, that one thinks, that a triangle is bounded by three lines.
  * *Deduction:* "the inference of something as following necessarily from other propositions which are known with certainty." It is a continuous, uninterrupted movement of thought that intuits each link.
  
  Long deductions depend on memory. *Enumeration* (Rules VII) is a rapid repeated survey of the chain, meant to reduce that dependence until the whole is grasped almost at once. This concern with memory reappears decisively in the Cartesian Circle.
* **Mathesis universalis (Rules IV):** Descartes proposes a "universal mathematics" that would explain "everything that can be raised concerning order and measure, irrespective of the subject matter." Arithmetic and geometry are not the method itself but its "outer garments." The program implies that any problem can be reduced to relations among quantities, once its order is properly arranged. *La Géométrie* is the most concrete realization of this idea.
* **Order and simple natures (Rules V–VI, XII):** The method consists in ordering objects so that complex and obscure ones are reduced step by step to simpler ones. Things are "absolute" when they contain the nature in question in its simplest form, and "relative" when they depend on the absolute. Rule XII introduces "simple natures," known per se and incapable of further analysis. They are of three kinds:
  * *Purely intellectual:* knowledge, doubt, volition.
  * *Purely material:* shape, extension, motion.
  * *Common:* existence, unity, duration, and common notions such as "things equal to a third are equal to each other."
  
  All human knowledge consists in intuiting simple natures and their combinations. Error arises only in composing them. This anticipates the later division between thinking and extended substance. It is also the first appearance of Descartes's view that the intellect, not the senses, grasps the basic elements of reality.
* **The four precepts (Discourse II):** The *Discourse* compresses the *Rules* into four precepts.
  1. Evidence: accept nothing as true that one does not know evidently to be so. Avoid haste (*précipitation*) and prejudice (*prévention*), and include in judgments nothing beyond what presents itself so clearly and distinctly that there is no occasion to doubt it.
  2. Division: divide each difficulty into as many parts as possible.
  3. Order: proceed from the simplest objects to the most complex, even supposing an order where none appears naturally.
  4. Enumeration: make complete enumerations and general reviews so that nothing is omitted.
  
  Descartes says he learned these from "the long chains of very simple and easy reasonings which geometers use" (AT VI 19).
* **Critique of syllogistic:** Syllogisms "serve rather to explain to others what one already knows than to learn anything" (Discourse II; compare Rules X). Formal validity cannot add knowledge. A syllogism presupposes its premises and therefore cannot discover. Descartes's alternative locates certainty in the intellect's direct grasp of necessary connections, not in the form of an argument. This shift moves logical authority from rules of form to the evidence of the knower, which is basic to his whole epistemology.
* **Analysis and synthesis (Second Replies, AT VII 155–159):** Descartes distinguishes two ways of demonstrating.
  * *Analysis* shows "the true way by means of which the thing in question was discovered methodically," so that a reader who follows it attentively makes the discovery his own.
  * *Synthesis* proceeds by definitions, postulates, axioms, and theorems, compelling assent even from a resistant reader. It does not reveal how the conclusions were found.
  
  Descartes says he used analysis in the *Meditations* because, in metaphysics, the first notions conflict with the prejudices of the senses. They are evident only to someone who has withdrawn the mind from the senses, which is a matter of meditation rather than of formal proof. In geometry, the first notions agree with the senses, so synthesis suits it. At Mersenne's request he nevertheless appended a synthetic exposition of the main arguments (AT VII 160–170). This distinction is the reason the *Meditations* must be read in their order. Their arguments are not a sequence of independent proofs but stages of cognitive self-transformation.
* **Method and experience:** Descartes is often caricatured as a pure apriorist, but the *Discourse* (VI, AT VI 63–65) says otherwise. The most general principles of nature can be derived from God and the nature of the mind. Particular effects, however, can be deduced from those principles in many possible ways. Experiments are needed to decide which way nature actually takes. As knowledge advances, experiments become *more* necessary, not less. His deductive method therefore fixes the framework of explanation, but it does not replace empirical inquiry into mechanisms. This is how he can practice detailed observation and dissection while affirming the deductive ideal.
* **Development:** In the *Rules*, intuition is treated as self-validating and is not itself called into doubt. In the *Meditations*, by contrast, even the simplest truths of arithmetic are made provisionally doubtful by the hypothesis of a deceiving God. The movement from the early method to the mature metaphysics is therefore a movement from presupposing the reliability of the intellect to trying to *vindicate* it. The first precept of the *Discourse* is generalized into the method of doubt. The success of that generalization is precisely what the problem of the Cartesian Circle calls into question.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Leibniz's emptiness objection:* The precepts are trivial unless one has a criterion for when something is clear and distinct. Leibniz compared them to a chemist's advice to "take what you need, do what you should, and you will get what you want."
  * *Fallible feeling of evidence:* The method seems to depend on a subjective experience of evidence, which can be mistaken.
  * *Unity of method:* Later philosophy of science doubted that a single method suits all domains. Aristotle's insistence that the degree of precision appropriate to a subject depends on its nature anticipates this criticism.
  * *Descartes's response:* The method is not a mechanical algorithm. It is a discipline of attention and order whose effectiveness is shown by its fruits, such as the geometrical results of the *Géométrie*.
* **Philosophical significance:** Descartes's method shaped several lasting ideals:
  * the unity of science;
  * epistemic foundationalism, in which knowledge rests on self-evident foundations reached by ordered inference;
  * the analytic decomposition of problems.
  
  It also shifted epistemic authority from tradition and formal logic to the individual knower's rational insight. This shift is basic to both modern rationalism and modern epistemology. The analysis/synthesis distinction also gives a model for reading philosophical texts as performances of inquiry rather than collections of theorems.

**Mathematics and Analytic Geometry**
* **Central thesis:** Geometrical problems can be solved systematically by representing geometrical magnitudes with algebraic symbols. Each problem is reduced to an equation, and the roots of the equation are then constructed geometrically with the simplest curves possible. *La Géométrie* (1637), one of the three essays published with the *Discourse*, is the clearest realization of the method of order and measure. Its philosophical interest lies in its account of exactness, representation, and the relation between algebra and geometry, not in algebra alone.
* **Arithmetic interpreted geometrically (Book I):** Descartes chooses a unit segment, which lets him interpret all five arithmetical operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and extraction of roots) as operations on line segments. The product of two lines is therefore another *line*, not an area. This removes a constraint that ancient geometry and Viète's algebra had observed: the requirement that terms in an equation have the same dimension, so that a square could not be added to a line. Freed from this "law of homogeneity," algebraic expressions of any degree could represent lengths. Descartes's notation became standard. Early letters (a, b, c) stand for known quantities, late letters (x, y, z) for unknowns, and exponents are written as superscripts (x³).
* **The Pappus problem:** The showcase of the *Géométrie* is the locus problem transmitted by Pappus. Given three, four, or more lines, find the locus of points such that the product of their distances (at given angles) to some of the lines bears a given ratio to the product of their distances to the others. Ancient geometers had solved only special cases. Descartes takes one given line as principal and measures every point by two quantities: a distance along that line and the length of a segment drawn at a fixed angle to it. He then expresses all the distances in terms of these two quantities and obtains an equation in two unknowns. For four lines, the locus is a conic section. This is the procedure later called "analytic geometry." Descartes's reference lines were generally oblique, his coordinates generally positive, and his choice of axes problem-specific. The term "Cartesian coordinates" refers to a later systematization.
* **Geometrical versus mechanical curves (Book II):** Descartes admits as genuinely geometrical only curves that can be traced by a continuous motion, or by several motions each completely determined by the preceding ones. Such curves can be known exactly. In modern terms, they correspond to curves expressible by algebraic equations. He excludes "mechanical" curves such as the spiral and the quadratrix, because they are generated by two independent motions—one rectilinear, one circular—"whose ratio we do not know exactly." He cites the ratio between straight and curved lines.
  * *Bos's interpretation:* Henk Bos argues that this demarcation shows Descartes defined geometrical exactness primarily by *construction* and only secondarily by algebraic equation. Algebra was the instrument of analysis. Exact constructibility, with curves of the lowest possible degree, was the criterion of geometrical legitimacy.
* **Normals, equations, and roots:** Book II also gives a method for finding the normal, and hence the tangent, to a curve at a point. One draws a circle meeting the curve at that point and imposes the condition that the resulting equation have a double root. Book III treats the theory of equations. It includes the rule of signs for estimating the number of positive and negative ("false") roots, and Descartes's term "imaginary" for roots that cannot be represented as quantities. It also covers the construction of roots of higher-degree equations by intersecting curves.
* **Relationship to method:** *Discourse* II says that Descartes wanted to combine the best of geometrical analysis and algebra while avoiding their defects. Ancient analysis was "so tied to figures" that it tired the imagination. Algebra was "so subject to rules and symbols" that it had become "a confused and obscure art." The *Géométrie* embodies the method's precepts. It divides a problem into its known and unknown quantities, orders them so that each depends on the preceding, and enumerates all the cases. It confirms Descartes's belief that the method is subject-neutral, since one procedure solves a whole family of problems that the ancients had treated case by case.
* **Relation to the metaphysics:** Mathematics plays two roles in the later system. It provides the model of certainty that metaphysics must vindicate: the simple truths of arithmetic are the first casualties of the deceiving-God doubt. And the "true and immutable natures" of geometrical figures provide the model for the ontological argument in *Meditation* V. Through the doctrine of the creation of the eternal truths, mathematical necessity becomes ultimately dependent on the divine will (see the God section).
* **Major objections:**
  * Fermat developed analytic geometry independently and contemporaneously. His *Introduction to Plane and Solid Loci* circulated around 1636–1637. The priority question is one of emphasis rather than invention.
  * Descartes's exclusion of mechanical curves proved untenable. The calculus of Newton and Leibniz showed that transcendental curves (spirals, cycloids, logarithmic curves) can be treated with full rigor. Leibniz explicitly criticized Descartes's restriction.
  * The *Géométrie* is notoriously compressed. Descartes deliberately omitted proofs and details, leaving "something for others to discover."
* **Philosophical significance:** The *Géométrie* transformed the concept of mathematical representation. Geometrical objects could now be known through algebraic structures, and algebraic expressions could be interpreted as magnitudes of any kind. This unification is an important historical precondition of the "mathematization of nature" and of the conception of matter as pure extension describable by quantities. Bos's analysis shows the significance of Descartes's own criterion. For him, the legitimacy of a mathematical object depended on whether it could be exactly constructed and represented. This makes his geometry an early, philosophically explicit theory of mathematical exactness, distinct from the later equation-based definitions.

**Methodological Skepticism**: `methodological skepticism`
* **Central thesis:** To establish anything "firm and lasting in the sciences," one must once in one's life "demolish everything completely and start again right from the foundations" (AT VII 17). Since it would be endless to examine each belief separately, one should attack the principles on which beliefs rest. Every belief that admits even the slightest doubt is treated provisionally as false. The doubt is a methodical instrument, not a skeptical conclusion. Its purpose is to "lead the mind away from the senses" (Synopsis, AT VII 12) and to find what survives any possible ground of doubt.
* **The apple-basket analogy:** Descartes explains his strategy in the Seventh Replies (AT VII 481). If you suspect some apples in a basket are rotten, you empty the whole basket. You then put back only those you have examined and found sound. Doubting everything is the means to a reliable selection, not the end.
* **Historical context:** The rediscovery of Sextus Empiricus (published in Latin in 1562) and the skeptical writings of Montaigne and Charron produced what Richard Popkin called a "Pyrrhonian crisis." It appeared in religious controversy, in "libertine" freethinking, and in natural philosophy. Descartes calls the skeptical arguments "stale cabbage" that he served up again only reluctantly (Second Replies, AT VII 130). His innovation is not the arguments but their use. Instead of yielding suspension of judgment as a way of life, they become a test. Only what survives the strongest grounds for doubt can serve as a foundation.
* **Stages of the doubt (Meditation I):**
  1. *Sensory error:* The senses sometimes deceive about small or distant objects, and "it is prudent never to trust completely those who have deceived us even once" (AT VII 18). This argument cannot reach beliefs such as "I am sitting by the fire in my dressing gown."
  2. *Madness:* Should I compare myself to madmen who think they are kings or made of glass? "They are insane, and I would be thought equally mad if I took them as a model." Michel Foucault read this dismissal as an exclusion of unreason from rational discourse, a reading Jacques Derrida contested. Most commentators read it as a dialectical step that the stronger dream argument supersedes.
  3. *Dreaming:* "I see plainly that there are never any sure signs by means of which being awake can be distinguished from being asleep" (AT VII 19). This raises doubt about all beliefs based on present sense experience, including beliefs about my own body. But just as painters must compose even fantastic creatures from real colors, the simplest and most universal elements—extension, shape, quantity, place, time—seem to survive the dream argument. So do arithmetic and geometry: "whether I am awake or asleep, two and three added together are five, and a square has no more than four sides" (AT VII 20).
  4. *The deceiving God:* "There is implanted in my mind a long-standing opinion that there is an omnipotent God who made me the kind of creature that I am." How do I know he has not made me err whenever I add two and three or count the sides of a square? If God's goodness forbids this, why does he permit me to be deceived occasionally? If there is no God and I am the product of fate, chance, or a series of causes, then "the less powerful they make my original cause, the more likely it is that I am so imperfect as to be deceived all the time." Either way, the reliability of my cognitive nature is uncertain. All former beliefs are therefore open to doubt "for powerful and well-thought-out reasons" (AT VII 21–22).
  5. *The malicious demon:* Habitual beliefs keep returning, so Descartes adopts a psychological device. He supposes "some malicious demon of the utmost power and cunning" employing all his energies to deceive him (AT VII 22–23). The demon is a means of sustaining the doubt, not a new ground for it. The deceiving-God argument contains the real epistemic force, because it concerns the origin of my nature.
* **The scope of doubt:** Does the doubt extend to perceptions that are clear and distinct *at the time they occur*? *Meditation* III (AT VII 36) says two things. Whenever I attend to things I perceive very clearly, "I am so convinced by them" that I cannot doubt them. Yet whenever "my preconceived belief in the supreme power of God comes to mind," I can doubt even what I seem to see most evidently. The doubt thus applies to clear perceptions only when we do not attend to them. Whether it reaches present perceptions *as such* is a central point in the debate on the Cartesian Circle.
* **The dream argument—reconstructions and problems:** One reconstruction runs:
  1. I have no criterion that distinguishes waking experience from dreaming.
  2. If I lack such a criterion, I cannot know that I am not now dreaming.
  3. If I cannot know that I am not now dreaming, I cannot know any proposition based on present sensory experience.
  4. So I cannot know any such proposition.
  
  Two objections are common.
  * *Self-undermining:* Knowing that dreams occur and resemble waking experience presupposes the very knowledge of waking experience the argument undermines. This objection is associated with J. L. Austin and Norman Malcolm. A reply is that the argument works as a *reductio* of the claim to sensory knowledge, using premises the believer accepts.
  * *The criterion requirement:* The requirement that knowledge needs a criterion one can recognize as such begs the question for internalism.
  
  Descartes himself answers the argument in *Meditation* VI (AT VII 89–90). Dreams are never connected by memory with the rest of my life, as waking experiences are. This answer is available only after God has been shown to be no deceiver.
* **Theoretical versus practical doubt:** The doubt is restricted to "the contemplation of the truth," not to action. In action we must follow probable opinions (Second Replies; Fifth Replies). This is why the *Discourse* (III) presents a "provisional morality" for living while doubting. The doubt is a deliberately "hyperbolical" exercise (AT VII 89), not a recommendation for ordinary life.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Bourdin (Seventh Objections):* Universal doubt undermines itself, since one can never recover from it.
  * *Gassendi (Fifth Objections):* It would be simpler and more honest to declare one's former beliefs uncertain without feigning their falsity.
  * *Peirce:* "Let us not pretend to doubt in philosophy what we do not doubt in our hearts." On this view, genuine doubt requires a positive reason, not a mere logical possibility.
  * *Wittgenstein (*On Certainty*):* Doubt presupposes a background of certainties that cannot themselves be meaningfully doubted.
  * *Frankfurt:* Descartes's doubt is not total. It targets the foundations of common-sense belief by testing their consistency with the principles that support them, and so it is dialectically limited by those principles.
  * *Descartes's response:* The doubt does not claim that the possibilities are likely. It requires only that they not be *excluded* by what we know. This is all a search for indubitable foundations requires.
* **Philosophical significance:** *Meditation* I gave classic form to two problems: external world skepticism and the problem of the criterion. The dream argument and the deceiving-God hypothesis are ancestors of the "brain in a vat" scenario (Putnam) and of contemporary debates about closure, contextualism, and the possibility of ruling out radical skeptical alternatives. The deceiving-God argument also introduced a distinctively modern problem. The reliability of one's own cognitive faculties depends on their origin, and that origin cannot be determined without already using those faculties. This problem resurfaces in Plantinga's evolutionary argument against naturalism and in debates about epistemic circularity.

**The Cogito and Self-Knowledge**
* **Central thesis:** Even under the most radical doubt, the existence of the thinking subject cannot be doubted. "Let him deceive me as much as he can, he will never bring it about that I am nothing so long as I think that I am something. So … this proposition, *I am, I exist*, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind" (AT VII 25). The formula "I think, therefore I am" appears in French in the *Discourse* (IV, AT VI 32) and in Latin in the *Principles* (I.7). The *Meditations* use "ego sum, ego existo." The certainty of one's existence as a thinking thing is the first item of knowledge in the order of reasons.
* **What the "I" is:** Having established *that* he exists, Descartes asks *what* he is (AT VII 25–29).
  * *Rejecting old definitions:* He rejects the Aristotelian definition "rational animal," because it would lead into further questions about "animal" and "rational." He also rejects his former view that he was a body with a soul that nourishes, moves, senses, and thinks.
  * *What survives:* Every attribute involving the body can be doubted. Thinking alone "cannot be separated from me." "I am, then, in the strict sense only a thing that thinks (*res cogitans*): that is, a mind, or intelligence, or intellect, or reason" (AT VII 27).
  * *The scope of "thought":* Thought is broad. A thinking thing is one that "doubts, understands, affirms, denies, is willing, is unwilling, and also imagines and has sensory perceptions" (AT VII 28).
  * *Seeming to perceive:* Even sensory perception, taken strictly, is certain. "I certainly seem to see, to hear, and to be warmed. This cannot be false; what is called 'having a sensory perception' is strictly just this" (*videre videor*, AT VII 29).
  * *Definition of thought:* The *Principles* (I.9) defines thought as "everything which we are aware of as happening within us, insofar as we have awareness of it."
* **The claim is epistemic, not yet metaphysical:** *Meditation* II does not conclude that the mind is a substance really distinct from the body. The objection was raised in the Preface to the Reader (AT VII 7–8) and in the Synopsis (AT VII 13). From the fact that I am aware of nothing belonging to my essence except thought, it does not follow that nothing else belongs to it. Descartes agrees. *Meditation* II establishes only that the mind can be known without knowing the body. The real distinction requires God's guarantee and is proved in *Meditation* VI. Conflating these steps is a common misreading.
* **Inference or intuition?** Is the cogito an inference with the suppressed major premise "whatever thinks exists"? Or is it something else?
  * *The textual evidence:* In the Second Replies (AT VII 140), Descartes denies that "I think, therefore I am" is a syllogism. If it were, he would first have to know the major premise. Instead, "he recognizes it as something self-evident by a simple intuition of the mind." General propositions are learned "from the fact that we experience in our own case that it is impossible to think without existing." The *Principles* (I.10), however, says that the cogito presupposes knowledge of "what thought, existence, and certainty are, and that it is impossible for that which thinks not to exist." Descartes calls these "very simple notions" that do not on their own provide knowledge of anything that exists. In the *Conversation with Burman*, reported speech of uncertain reliability, he is said to allow that the general premise can be known implicitly before the particular conclusion.
  * *Interpretations:*
    * *Implicit inference:* The cogito is an inference with an implicit general premise, known as a common notion.
    * *Intuition:* The cogito is a single immediate intuition of the necessary connection between my thinking and my existing.
    * *Performance (Hintikka):* The certainty of "I exist" derives from the *existential inconsistency* of the statement "I do not exist." Uttering or thinking it falsifies it. The formula "whenever it is put forward by me" supports this reading. Its weakness is that Descartes's cogito works for any mental act ("I doubt," "I seem to see"), not just for saying or thinking "I exist."
* **Gassendi's objection and the special status of thought:** Gassendi asked why "I walk, therefore I am" is not equally good (Fifth Objections). Descartes replies (Fifth Replies, AT VII 352) that it is good only as "I am aware of seeming to walk." Walking is a bodily act, so it is doubtful. The awareness of walking is a thought, so it is certain. Thought's privilege is its self-presentation. My thinking cannot be present to me as an object of doubt without my thinking.
* **The objection from the subject (Lichtenberg, Russell):** "I think" assumes a subject, an enduring self, beyond the occurrence of thinking. Lichtenberg suggested one should say "it thinks," as one says "it lightens." Russell argued that the certain datum is only "there is thinking." In the Third Objections (AT VII 172–174), Hobbes argued that the thinking thing might be corporeal, since a thing distinct from its acts may be a body. Descartes replies (Third Replies, AT VII 175–176) that acts and attributes cannot exist without a substance in which they inhere. This presupposes the principle that "nothingness has no attributes" (*Principles* I.11, I.52). It is a substantive metaphysical principle the critic may reject. Kant's Paralogisms carry the criticism further. The "I think" expresses the formal unity of consciousness, which accompanies all representations. It yields no cognition of the subject as a substance.
* **The wax argument (AT VII 30–34):**
  * *Purpose:* The meditator still tends to think that bodies, which are perceived by the senses and imagination, are known more distinctly than the mind. The wax argument is meant to correct this tendency. It shows that even bodies are known by the intellect, and that every act of knowing a body confirms the mind's own existence more clearly.
  * *The argument:* Freshly taken from the honeycomb, the wax has a particular taste, scent, color, shape, and size, and makes a sound when struck. Brought near the fire, it changes in every one of these qualities, yet "the same wax remains." What remains is "merely something extended, flexible and changeable." Imagination cannot grasp it, because it cannot run through the innumerable changes of shape the wax can undergo. The wax is therefore perceived "by the mind alone" (*solius mentis inspectio*). Similarly, looking from a window at people in the street, I see only hats and coats, which might conceal automata. I *judge* that they are people.
  * *Conclusions:* Perception of bodies is, at its core, a judgment of the intellect, not a mere sensory reception. And every such judgment, whether true or false, establishes my existence more certainly than the wax's (AT VII 33).
  * *Interpretive note:* The wax passage does not yet assert that bodies exist. That is established in *Meditation* VI. It anticipates the later identification of body's essence with extension, which is grasped by intellect.
* **Self-transparency:** "There can be nothing in the mind, insofar as it is a thinking thing, of which it is not aware" (Fourth Replies, AT VII 246). This seems to imply that all mental states are conscious and available to introspection. In the same reply, however, Descartes adds that we are always aware of our *acts* of thought, but not necessarily of our *faculties* or powers. Elsewhere he allows that we may not reflectively remember or attend to our acts. Interpreters disagree whether he held a strong transparency thesis or a weaker thesis that mental states are essentially conscious, though not necessarily reflectively known.
* **Augustinian background:** In the Fourth Objections (AT VII 197), Arnauld noted that Augustine had used a similar argument ("if I am mistaken, I exist"). In a letter to Colvius of 1640 (AT III 247–248), Descartes acknowledged the parallel but distinguished his use of it. Augustine used it to show certainty of existence and to find in the mind an image of the Trinity. Descartes used it to establish that the "I" is an immaterial thinking thing and as the first principle of his philosophy.
* **Major objections:**
  * The step from "I exist" to "I am a thinking thing" relies on the principle that whatever can be doubted cannot belong to my essence, which is invalid as a metaphysical inference.
  * The certainty of the cogito may extend only to momentary thinking and not to an enduring self. Descartes says, "I am, I exist—that is certain. But for how long? For as long as I am thinking" (AT VII 27).
  * The cogito presupposes a substance-attribute metaphysics.
* **Philosophical significance:** The cogito gave the first-person perspective a foundational status in epistemology. This defined modern debates about self-knowledge, introspection, and the privileged access of subjects to their own minds. The discussions it provoked include:
  * Kant's transcendental unity of apperception;
  * Husserl's phenomenological reduction, which he described as a radicalized Cartesian method in his *Cartesian Meditations*;
  * Wittgenstein's and Anscombe's analyses of the first person;
  * contemporary work on immunity to error through misidentification (Shoemaker).
  
  The wax argument also introduced the thesis that perception involves judgment.

**Ideas, Innateness, and Rationalism**: `innatism`, `representationalism (contested)`
* **Central thesis:** Ideas are forms of thought through which the mind represents things. Every idea, considered as a mode of the mind, has the same "formal reality." But ideas differ in "objective reality," in what and how much they represent. Some ideas are innate: they are derived from the mind's own nature rather than from sense experience or invention. These include the ideas of thinking, of extension, of truth, and above all of God. Innateness is primarily a disposition to form ideas, not a stock of actually present contents.
* **Definitions of "idea":** Descartes uses "idea" in several senses.
  * *Narrow sense:* "Some of my thoughts are as it were the images of things, and it is only in these cases that the term 'idea' is strictly appropriate" (AT VII 37).
  * *Broad sense:* In the Second Replies (def. II, AT VII 160), an idea is "the form of any given thought, immediate perception of which makes me aware of the thought."
  * *Material and objective senses:* In the Preface to the Reader (AT VII 8), "idea" can be taken "materially," as an operation of the intellect, or "objectively," as the thing represented by that operation.
  
  Ideas, considered in themselves, cannot be false, since I can as truly imagine a goat as a chimera. Falsity lies in judgment, when I judge that ideas resemble or conform to things outside me.
* **Formal and objective reality:** Descartes adapts scholastic terminology.
  * *Formal reality:* the reality a thing has as an existing thing.
  * *Objective reality:* the reality of what an idea represents, insofar as it is "objectively in the intellect." It is "the being of the thing represented insofar as it is in the intellect, in the way in which objects are normally there" (First Replies, AT VII 102).
  
  He also assigns degrees of reality: infinite substance, then finite substance, then modes (AT VII 40; Third Replies, AT VII 185). The idea of God therefore has more objective reality than the idea of a stone. This apparatus is the basis of the trademark argument (see God below).
* **Classification of ideas (AT VII 37–38):** Ideas seem to fall into three classes.
  * *Innate:* derived from my own nature, such as what a thing is, what truth is, and what thought is.
  * *Adventitious:* coming from outside, such as hearing a noise, seeing the sun, or feeling heat.
  * *Invented:* made up by me, such as sirens and hippogriffs.
  
  Descartes says that perhaps all are innate, or all adventitious, or all invented, "for I have not yet clearly perceived their true origin." The classification is provisional at this stage of the *Meditations*.
* **Why sensory ideas are not trusted:** Two reasons incline us to think that adventitious ideas resemble their causes (AT VII 38–40). Nature seems to teach it, and these ideas come to us independently of our will. But "nature" here means a spontaneous impulse, not the natural light. Ideas also come to us involuntarily in dreams. Descartes illustrates the gap between sensory ideas and reality with the sun. I have two ideas of it. One comes from the senses and makes it appear small. The other comes from astronomical reasoning and shows it to be many times larger than the earth. They cannot both resemble the sun, and reason shows that the one least resembling it is the one coming from the senses.
* **Innateness as disposition (Comments on a Certain Broadsheet, 1648):** Henricus Regius argued that the mind needs no innate ideas. Descartes replied that he never wrote that ideas are actually innate as distinct from the faculty of thinking. He said innate ideas are innate in the way generosity, or diseases such as gout and gravel, are innate in some families. Babies in such families are not sick in the womb, but they are born with a disposition to contract these conditions (AT VIIIB 357–358). Descartes also argues that, in a sense, *all* ideas, including sensory ones, are innate. Nothing reaches the mind from external objects except corporeal motions, and neither the motions nor the shapes produced by them are conceived by us as they occur in the sense organs. The mind must therefore produce the ideas of colors, sounds, and pains on the occasion of these motions (AT VIIIB 358–359). This broad innatism follows from the mechanistic theory of perception: nothing in the physical stimulus resembles the sensory idea.
* **Material falsity (AT VII 43–44):** There is a kind of falsity in ideas themselves, called material falsity, when they "represent a non-thing as a thing." The idea of cold is so obscure that I cannot tell whether cold is a real quality or merely the absence of heat. If cold is a privation, the idea that represents it as something real is materially false.
  * *Arnauld's objection (Fourth Objections, AT VII 206–207):* An idea is the thing itself insofar as it is objectively in the intellect. If cold is a privation, the idea of cold represents a privation and so is not false. If it represents something positive, it is not the idea of cold.
  * *Descartes's reply (AT VII 232–235):* Material falsity consists in the idea's providing the judgment with material for error. This reply seems to retreat from the claim that ideas themselves are false.
  * *Interpretations:* Margaret Wilson distinguished an idea's "representational character," how it presents its object, from its "referential" relation to an actual cause. Material falsity arises when an obscure idea presents itself as representing something real while its actual object is something else or nothing.
* **The representationalism debate:** Are Descartes's ideas mental objects, perceived *instead of* external things? Or are they mental acts through which we perceive things directly?
  * *The Arnauld–Malebranche dispute:* In *On True and False Ideas* (1683), Arnauld argued that ideas are perceptions, acts of the mind, not representative beings distinct from them. Malebranche held that we see ideas in God, as entities distinct from our perceptions.
  * *Reid's critique:* Thomas Reid later attacked the "theory of ideas" as leading inevitably to skepticism.
  * *Yolton and others:* Some argue that Descartes's ideas are not intermediaries. They are the ways in which the mind is cognitively related to things.
  
  The "objective reality" apparatus implies that ideas have a representational content distinct from their existence as acts. It does not by itself imply that we perceive ideas rather than things.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Locke's criticism of innatism (Essay I):* No proposition can be in the mind without being known. If capacity alone counts as innateness, all truths the mind ever comes to know would be innate, making the doctrine trivial. Descartes's dispositional version answers the first point but faces the second.
  * *Hobbes and Gassendi:* We have no idea of God or of the mind in the proper sense. We have only images, or names that do not correspond to ideas (Third and Fifth Objections).
  * *Descartes's reply:* Ideas are not images. The idea of God or of thought is understood by the intellect, not represented by the imagination. Hobbes simply equates ideas with images.
* **Philosophical significance:** The distinction between formal and objective reality is a key ancestor of the modern concept of representational content and of intentionality. Brentano drew on the scholastic "intentional inexistence" that Descartes inherited. Descartes's dispositional innatism set the terms for the rationalism–empiricism debate (Locke, Leibniz's *New Essays*). Noam Chomsky's appeal to "Cartesian linguistics" as an ancestor of generative grammar illustrates its later uses, although historians have questioned the accuracy of that genealogy. Descartes's view that sensory ideas are produced by the mind on the occasion of non-resembling stimuli became a core assumption of modern perceptual psychology.

**God and the Foundations of Knowledge**: `theism`, `divine voluntarism`
* **Central thesis:** The existence of God can be demonstrated by reason alone. One argument proceeds from the idea of God in my mind and from my own existence (*Meditation* III). The other proceeds from God's essence (*Meditation* V). God is supremely perfect and therefore no deceiver. God's existence and veracity are thus the foundation of all knowledge beyond present, attentive intuition, of all *scientia*. God is also the free creator even of the eternal truths of mathematics and logic.
* **The causal principle (AT VII 40–41):** "It is manifest by the natural light that there must be at least as much reality in the efficient and total cause as in the effect of that cause." From this it follows both that something cannot come from nothing and that what is more perfect cannot come from what is less perfect. The principle applies to ideas' objective reality. The cause of an idea must contain "formally or eminently" at least as much reality as the idea contains objectively.
  * *Formal containment:* The cause contains the very perfection represented.
  * *Eminent containment:* The cause contains it in a higher form.
  
  Although one idea can be caused by another, the regress cannot be infinite. It must end in a primary idea whose cause contains formally all the reality the idea contains objectively.
* **The trademark argument (AT VII 45–52):**
  1. I have an idea of God: "a substance that is infinite, eternal, immutable, independent, supremely intelligent, supremely powerful, and which created both myself and everything else."
  2. This idea has infinite objective reality.
  3. The cause of an idea must contain formally or eminently at least as much reality as the idea contains objectively.
  4. I am a finite substance and cannot contain infinite reality.
  5. Hence the cause of my idea of God is a being that contains infinite reality formally: God exists.
  
  Descartes anticipates objections.
  * *The infinite is not derived by negation:* The idea of the infinite is not derived by negating the finite. On the contrary, the perception of the infinite is "somehow prior" to that of the finite, because I could not know that I doubt and desire, and so lack something, without the idea of a more perfect being.
  * *Clarity without comprehension:* The idea of God is "utterly clear and distinct" and contains more objective reality than any other, so it cannot be materially false. Though I cannot "grasp" (*comprehendere*) the infinite, I can "understand" (*intelligere*) it.
  * *The image:* God placed this idea in me "to be, as it were, the mark of the craftsman stamped on his work" (AT VII 51). This is the source of the name "trademark argument."
* **The argument from my existence (AT VII 47–51):** A second argument asks what causes *me*, a being that has the idea of God.
  * *Not myself:* If I were the author of my own being, I would have given myself all the perfections I lack.
  * *Not having always existed:* The parts of time are independent. That I existed a moment ago does not entail that I exist now, unless some cause creates me anew. Conservation is the same as creation, differing only in our way of thinking (AT VII 49).
  * *Not my parents or a series of causes:* Whatever caused me must have the idea of God and must conserve me. Any such cause must ultimately be God.
  
  This argument is not independent of the first, since it also depends on my having the idea of God. It adds the doctrine of continuous creation, which is basic to Descartes's physics.
* **Objections to the causal argument:**
  * *Caterus (First Objections, AT VII 92):* The objective being of an idea is a mere "extrinsic denomination" of the thing, not something real, so it needs no cause.
  * *Descartes's reply (AT VII 102–104):* Objective being is a real mode of being, though less perfect than formal being. If someone has the idea of a very ingenious machine, we ask its cause: did he see such a machine, or did he possess great skill in mechanics?
  * *Hobbes (Third Objections, AT VII 180):* We have no idea of God, only a name and a set of images.
  * *Gassendi (Fifth Objections):* The idea of the infinite can be constructed by amplifying finite perfections.
  * *Later critics (Hume, Kant):* Hume argued that the idea of God arises from augmenting ideas of human qualities without limit. Kant criticized the causal principle's extension beyond experience.
  
  Even sympathetic commentators find the premise that the cause of an idea's representational content must contain an equal amount of "reality" the least plausible element. It applies a scholastic principle of causal adequacy to representational content, where it is far from self-evident.
* **The ontological argument (Meditation V, AT VII 63–69):**
  * *The model of true and immutable natures:* Geometrical figures such as the triangle have "true and immutable natures" whose properties are discovered, not invented. That its angles equal two right angles follows from its nature whether I like it or not.
  * *The argument:*
    1. Whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive to belong to the true and immutable nature of a thing can truly be affirmed of that thing.
    2. I clearly and distinctly perceive that existence belongs to the nature of a supremely perfect being.
    3. Therefore God exists.
  * *The analogy:* Existence can no more be separated from God's essence than the fact that its three angles equal two right angles can be separated from the essence of a triangle, or the idea of a valley from the idea of a mountain.
  * *The "necessity of the thing" objection:* Descartes raises it himself. My thought imposes no necessity on things. From the fact that I cannot think of a winged horse without wings, it does not follow that a winged horse exists. He replies that the case of God is different. Here "the necessity of the thing itself, namely the existence of God, determines my thinking" (AT VII 67).
  * *Synthetic version:* In the Second Replies' synthetic presentation, the ontological argument becomes the first proposition (AT VII 166). This suggests that Descartes regarded it as the more fundamental proof in the order of synthesis.
* **Objections to the ontological argument:**
  * *Caterus (First Objections, AT VII 99):* Echoing Aquinas, Caterus argues that from the concept of a supremely perfect being it follows only that the *concept* includes existence, not that the being actually exists.
  * *Descartes's reply (First Replies, AT VII 115–119):* He distinguishes possible existence, contained in the idea of every clearly conceived thing, from necessary existence, contained only in the idea of God. He also distinguishes true and immutable natures from fictitious ones, which the mind can divide.
  * *Gassendi (Fifth Objections, AT VII 323):* Existence is not a perfection or property. It is that without which there are no properties. This objection anticipates Kant's claim that "being is not a real predicate." Descartes's reply (AT VII 382–383) is brief: existence is a property in the same sense as any other attribute.
  * *Leibniz:* The argument is incomplete until it is shown that the idea of a supremely perfect being is possible, that is, contains no contradiction. Descartes had suggested in the Second Replies (AT VII 150–151) that the idea's clarity and distinctness guarantees its consistency. Leibniz did not accept this.
  * *Contemporary versions:* Modern modal versions, such as Plantinga's, show that the argument's force depends on the premise that a maximally great being is possible. This premise is as contested as the conclusion.
* **Divine veracity:** God is supremely perfect. Fraud and deception depend on some defect, such as malice or weakness. So God cannot be a deceiver (AT VII 52–53). It follows that the faculty of judgment God gave me cannot lead me into error when I use it correctly. But God *permits* me to err occasionally, so *Meditation* IV must explain error without making God responsible for it (see the next entry).
* **The creation of the eternal truths:** Descartes holds that mathematical truths and logical necessities are not independent of God. In letters to Mersenne of 15 April, 6 May, and 27 May 1630 (AT I 145–153), he writes that "the mathematical truths which you call eternal have been laid down by God and depend on him entirely no less than the rest of his creatures." God established them as a king establishes laws in his kingdom. God was free to make it not true that all the radii of a circle are equal. God's will, understanding, and creating are one and the same act. One should not say that "if God did not exist nevertheless these truths would be true."
  * *Later texts:* To Mesland (2 May 1644, AT IV 118–119), Descartes says that God could have made contradictories true together, even though we cannot conceive this. To Arnauld (29 July 1648, AT V 223–224), he says that he dares not claim God cannot make a mountain without a valley or one and two not make three. He holds only that God has given him a mind that cannot conceive such things. The Sixth Replies (AT VII 432, 435–436) reaffirm that God did not will the angles of a triangle to equal two right angles because he knew it could not be otherwise. Rather, because he willed it, it is true.
  * *Motivation:* Against the scholastic view, associated with Suárez, that essences are eternally true independently of God's will, Descartes affirms God's absolute sovereignty and the dependence of everything on him.
  * *Interpretations:*
    * *Universal possibilism (Frankfurt):* God could have done the logically impossible. Descartes holds that the eternal truths are necessary but not necessarily necessary.
    * *Iterated modality (Curley):* The doctrine denies the S5 principle. What is necessary might not have been necessary, since God freely made it so, but given his decree, it is necessary.
    * *Conceivability-based modality (Bennett and others):* Necessity is relative to the structure of our minds, which God created. The doctrine expresses the claim that our modal concepts do not constrain God.
  * *Tensions:* If the eternal truths depend on God's free will, it is unclear how they can be "immutable natures" that ground the ontological argument. One reply is that God's decrees are themselves immutable, so the truths are immutable *given* his will. It is also unclear how the truths can be known a priori. And the doctrine threatens the Cartesian Circle: if logical necessity depends on God's free will, can even our clearest perceptions of necessity be trusted without God's guarantee?
* **Philosophical significance:** The trademark argument links representational content to causal explanation. This connection is basic to later causal theories of content and to debates about the origin of concepts. The ontological argument in its Cartesian form provoked Kant's critique and its modern modal reformulations. Divine veracity is the first sustained attempt to justify the reliability of human cognition by appeal to its source. It is the ancestor of reliabilist and evolutionary debates about how the origin of our faculties bears on their trustworthiness. The doctrine of the eternal truths raises enduring questions about the source of necessity, the relation of logic to God, and the limits of conceivability as a guide to possibility.

**Freedom, Will, and the Problem of Error**: `doxastic voluntarism`
* **Central thesis:** Error is not caused by God or by any positive defect in my faculties. It arises from the relation between two faculties, both good in themselves. The intellect is finite. The will is infinite in scope and extends to matters I do not clearly understand. Judgment is an act of will: affirming or denying what the intellect presents. I err when I affirm or deny what I do not clearly and distinctly perceive. Since I can always suspend judgment in such cases, error is my own responsibility. The rule that follows, to assent only to what I clearly and distinctly perceive, guarantees that I will never err. This is what vindicates the truth rule.
* **The problem:** If God is supremely good and no deceiver, and my faculty of judgment comes from God, why do I ever err? Error is not a mere negation, a lack of something that does not belong to me. It is a privation, "the lack of some knowledge which somehow should be in me" (AT VII 55). Descartes's reasoning has the structure of a theodicy. He begins with two considerations.
  * *Divine purposes:* We cannot understand God's purposes, so it would be rash to seek final causes in physics (AT VII 55).
  * *The whole:* A creature that seems imperfect in isolation may be perfect when considered as part of the universe.
* **Intellect and will:**
  * *Intellect:* Through it, I merely perceive ideas about which I can make judgments. It affirms and denies nothing.
  * *Will:* It is the power of affirming or denying, pursuing or avoiding. Judgment is an act of will (AT VII 56; *Principles* I.32–34).
  
  This makes Descartes a doxastic voluntarist: belief is an exercise of the will. The intellect is finite: there are innumerable things of which I have no idea. The will is not restricted in this way: "It is only the will, or freedom of choice, which I experience within me to be so great that the idea of any greater faculty is beyond my grasp. So it is above all in virtue of the will that I understand myself to bear in some way the image and likeness of God" (AT VII 57). The will is not greater in God than in me in its formal nature. It is greater in its knowledge and power, which make it firmer and more efficacious.
* **The definition of freedom (AT VII 57–58):** "The will simply consists in our ability to do or not to do something (that is, to affirm or deny, to pursue or avoid); or rather, it consists simply in the fact that when the intellect puts something forward for affirmation or denial or for pursuit or avoidance, our inclinations are such that we do not feel we are determined by any external force." The definition contains two clauses that may not coincide.
  * *Two-way power:* The ability to do or not do.
  * *Spontaneity:* The absence of a felt external determination.
* **Indifference and the degrees of freedom:** Descartes immediately adds that indifference—the state in which no reason pushes me one way rather than another—is "the lowest grade of freedom." It reflects a defect in knowledge, not a perfection of will. "In order to be free, there is no need for me to be capable of going in each of two directions; on the contrary, the more I incline in one direction—either because I clearly understand that reasons of truth and goodness point that way, or because of a divinely produced disposition of my inmost thoughts—the freer is my choice. Neither divine grace nor natural knowledge ever diminishes freedom; on the contrary, they increase and strengthen it" (AT VII 57–58). When I clearly perceive that something is true, "a great light in the intellect was followed by a great inclination in the will" (AT VII 59). Clear perception is thus followed necessarily by assent. Yet that assent is maximally free, since freedom consists in spontaneous, reason-guided inclination.
* **The source of error and the truth rule:** "The scope of the will is wider than that of the intellect; but instead of restricting it within the same limits, I extend its use to matters which I do not understand" (AT VII 58). Error is the misuse of freedom. It is not a positive reality created by God. The privation is in the act of judgment insofar as it proceeds from me (AT VII 60). The remedy is to withhold assent whenever I do not clearly and distinctly perceive. Descartes then draws the conclusion that vindicates the truth rule. "Every clear and distinct perception is undoubtedly something, and hence cannot come from nothing, but must necessarily have God for its author." God is supremely perfect and no deceiver. Hence such a perception "is undoubtedly true" (AT VII 62). This is the formal justification of the rule announced provisionally in *Meditation* III.
* **The Mesland letters and the "two-way power":** The *Meditations* seem to say that clear perception necessitates assent. Letters to Denis Mesland seem to say otherwise.
  * *2 May 1644 (AT IV 115–118):* Descartes distinguishes indifference as a state of uncertainty from a positive power of determining oneself to either of two contraries.
  * *9 February 1645 (AT IV 173–175):* "When a very evident reason moves us in one direction, although morally speaking we can hardly move in the opposite direction, absolutely speaking we can. For it is always open to us to hold back from pursuing a clearly known good, or from admitting a clearly perceived truth, provided we consider it a good thing to demonstrate the freedom of our will by so doing."
  * *Interpretations:*
    * *Inconsistency (Kenny):* Descartes held two incompatible conceptions of freedom.
    * *Reconciliation (Ragland and others):* At the moment of clear perception, one cannot dissent. But one can turn one's attention away from the reasons, and then withhold assent. On this reading, the two-way power operates through attention, not through direct dissent from present evidence.
  
  The question bears on whether Descartes's theory is compatibilist, since freedom is consistent with determination by reasons, or libertarian, since freedom requires a power of alternatives.
* **Freedom and divine preordination:** The *Principles* (I.39–41) state that our freedom is self-evident and that we are conscious of it. God's preordination of all things is also certain. How the two are reconciled is beyond our finite comprehension. It would be absurd to doubt what we experience within ourselves merely because we cannot grasp something that is by its nature incomprehensible.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Gassendi (Fifth Objections):* The intellect and will have the same scope. We can only will what we understand in some way.
  * *Spinoza (Ethics II, prop. 49, scholium):* Will and intellect are one and the same. To have an idea is to affirm it, unless it is limited by another idea. There is no separate faculty of will that adds assent to an idea.
  * *Against direct doxastic voluntarism (Hume, Williams):* Belief is not under direct voluntary control. Bernard Williams argued that if one could believe at will, regardless of evidence, the resulting state would not be belief. Descartes's position can be defended as an indirect voluntarism. We control assent by directing attention, inquiry, and the suspension of judgment.
  * *The theodicy objection:* God could have given me a clearer intellect, or a will that assents only to clear perceptions. Descartes replies (AT VII 60–62) that I have no right to complain that God did not give me more, and that the universe may be more perfect containing beings like me.
* **Philosophical significance:** Descartes's theory of judgment as an act of will raises questions that remain central to epistemology:
  * epistemic responsibility;
  * the ethics of belief (Clifford, James);
  * whether belief is voluntary;
  * the distinction between entertaining and endorsing a content.
  
  His account of freedom as maximal when guided by clear reasons is a predecessor of rationalist and compatibilist theories (Spinoza, Leibniz, Kant). His positive two-way power anticipates libertarian theories. His ethics of *générosité*, based on the right use of free will, depends directly on this account.

**The Criterion of Truth**: `rationalism`
* **Central thesis:** Whatever is clearly and distinctly perceived is true. This truth rule is not simply assumed. It is announced as provisional in *Meditation* III and justified by God's non-deceiving nature and the theory of judgment in *Meditation* IV. Clear and distinct perception gives rise to *scientia*: a certainty that cannot be shaken by any stronger reason. Mere conviction, or *persuasio*, can be overturned. Not every strongly held belief is clearly and distinctly perceived. The criterion is not a psychological feeling of certainty, but a perception of a specific kind.
* **Clarity and distinctness (Principles I.45–46):**
  * *Clear:* "present and accessible to the attentive mind—just as we say that we see something clearly when it is present to the eye's gaze and stimulates it with a sufficient degree of strength and accessibility."
  * *Distinct:* "so sharply separated from all other perceptions that it contains within itself only what is clear."
  
  A perception can be clear without being distinct. When someone feels an intense pain, the perception of pain is very clear. It is not distinct, because people usually confuse it with an obscure judgment about the nature of something in the painful part of the body that resembles the sensation. But a perception cannot be distinct without being clear.
* **The truth rule:** *Meditation* III (AT VII 35) introduces the rule tentatively: "I now seem to be able to lay down as a general rule that whatever I perceive very clearly and distinctly is true." Its status is immediately qualified by the possibility of a deceiving God. It is vindicated in *Meditation* IV (AT VII 62) and reaffirmed in *Meditation* V (AT VII 70–71): "the certainty and truth of all knowledge depends uniquely on my awareness of the true God."
* **What clear and distinct perception yields:** It yields:
  * the simple natures;
  * the common notions or axioms, such as "nothing comes from nothing" and "what is done cannot be undone" (*Principles* I.49);
  * the true and immutable natures of geometrical figures and of God;
  * the essence of body as extension (*Meditation* V; *Principles* II.4);
  * the essence of mind as thought.
  
  Sensory ideas, by contrast, are mostly obscure and confused. They tell us what is beneficial or harmful for the union of mind and body, not the nature of bodies (see the next installment).
* **Scientia and persuasio:** In a letter to Regius of 24 May 1640 (AT III 64–65), Descartes distinguishes the two.
  * *Persuasio:* There remains some reason that might lead us to doubt.
  * *Scientia:* "conviction based on a reason so strong that it can never be shaken by any stronger reason."
  
  This distinction explains the claim in the Second Replies (AT VII 141) that an atheist geometer can clearly know that the angles of a triangle equal two right angles. That knowledge is not true *scientia*, because a doubt about his origin can always be raised. *Scientia* is therefore systematic. It requires the integration of individual clear perceptions into a whole secured by knowledge of God.
* **Absolute certainty (Second Replies, AT VII 144–145):** Descartes writes that "there is a conviction so firm that it is quite incapable of being destroyed; and such a conviction is clearly the same as the most perfect certainty." He then asks: "What is it to us that someone may make out that the perception whose truth we are so firmly convinced of may appear false to God or an angel, so that it is, absolutely speaking, false? Why should this alleged 'absolute falsity' bother us, since we neither believe in it nor have even the smallest suspicion of it?" This passage is the main textual basis for the "coherence" reading of Descartes's epistemology (Frankfurt). On that reading, his goal is unshakable certainty rather than a demonstration of correspondence with reality.
* **Rational certainty versus psychological conviction:** A madman or a fanatic can be psychologically certain. What distinguishes clear and distinct perception? Descartes's answer is that clarity and distinctness concern the *content* and *mode* of a perception. A perception is clear when its object is fully present to an attentive mind. It is distinct when nothing confused is mixed with it. Psychological conviction may accompany obscure perceptions, as with prejudices from childhood. The difference can be discovered by analysis, by separating what is perceived from what is merely assumed. Descartes admits that we can be mistaken about whether a perception is clear and distinct. In the Seventh Replies (AT VII 461–462), he says that it takes care to distinguish what is clearly perceived from what merely seems so.
* **Moral and absolute certainty:** Not all knowledge reaches metaphysical certainty. The *Principles* (IV.205–206) distinguish moral certainty, sufficient for the conduct of life, from absolute certainty. Many explanations in physics have only moral certainty. Descartes's analogy is decoding a cipher: if a key yields a meaningful text, it is morally certain to be the correct one, though another key might in principle also work. Others, such as the claim that bodies exist and that their essence is extension, have absolute certainty.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Gassendi (Fifth Objections, AT VII 278–279):* Many people are convinced that they clearly perceive what is false. A method is needed to determine when a perception really is clear and distinct. Descartes replies (Fifth Replies, AT VII 379) that his *Meditations* provide this method.
  * *Leibniz:* In "Meditations on Knowledge, Truth, and Ideas" (1684), Leibniz argued that clarity and distinctness are useless criteria without marks by which to recognize them. He proposed instead a graded classification of knowledge: obscure or clear; if clear, confused or distinct; if distinct, inadequate or adequate; symbolic or intuitive. Distinct knowledge requires the ability to enumerate the marks that distinguish a thing.
  * *Internalism:* The criterion is internalist: it depends on features of perceptions accessible to the knower. Externalists argue that no internal feature can guarantee truth, so the criterion either fails or needs God's guarantee to do what it is supposed to do.
* **Philosophical significance:** Descartes's truth rule is the classic statement of rationalist foundationalism. It holds that certain cognitions are self-evident and that their evidence is the mark of truth. The *scientia*/*persuasio* distinction anticipates the contemporary distinction between knowledge as a lower-level state and reflective, systematically integrated knowledge. Ernest Sosa, for example, uses Descartes to distinguish "animal" from "reflective" knowledge. The question how to distinguish rational certainty from conviction remains central to debates on intuition and self-evidence.

**The Cartesian Circle**
* **Central problem:** Descartes seems to rely on clear and distinct perception to prove that God exists. He also relies on God's existence to guarantee that whatever is clearly and distinctly perceived is true. Arnauld put the problem precisely in the Fourth Objections (AT VII 214): "I have one further worry, namely how the author avoids reasoning in a circle when he says that we are sure that what we clearly and distinctly perceive is true only because God exists. But we can be sure that God exists only because we clearly and evidently perceive this." Mersenne raised a related worry about the atheist geometer (Second Objections, AT VII 124–125).
* **Descartes's replies:** Descartes responds several times.
  * *Second Replies (AT VII 140–141, 145–146):* The atheist can clearly know geometrical truths, but this knowledge is not true *scientia*, since he can be led to doubt when not attending to the proof. Some perceptions are "so transparently clear and at the same time so simple that we cannot ever think of them without believing them to be true."
  * *Fourth Replies (AT VII 245–246):* Descartes distinguishes "what we in fact perceive clearly and what we remember having perceived clearly on a previous occasion." The certainty of present clear perception does not depend on God. Only the certainty of conclusions we remember having proved does.
  * *Meditation V (AT VII 69–70):* When I am no longer attending to the arguments by which I perceived some truth, I can doubt it if I am ignorant of God. Once I know God, I am certain of it "as long as I remember that I clearly and distinctly perceived it," even if I no longer attend to the reasons.
* **Central interpretations:**
  1. *The memory interpretation (Doney):* God's existence is needed only to guarantee memories that we once had clear and distinct perceptions. Present clear and distinct perceptions need no guarantee.
     * *Strength:* It matches the Fourth Replies and *Meditation* V.
     * *Weaknesses:* The proof of God is itself long and depends on memory. *Meditation* III explicitly extends doubt to simple matters such as "two and three make five." And God does not guarantee the reliability of memory in general, since we often misremember.
  2. *The general rule versus particular perceptions (Van Cleve):* Each particular clear and distinct perception is certain while it is had. What God guarantees is the *general rule* that whatever is clearly and distinctly perceived is true. The proof of God uses particular clear perceptions, not the general rule, so there is no circle.
     * *Strength:* It distinguishes the epistemic status of individual perceptions from that of the principle about them. It also matches the logic of epistemic principles: one can know something by a reliable process without knowing that the process is reliable.
     * *Weakness:* *Meditation* III suggests that even particular clear perceptions can be doubted when one considers God's possible power to deceive.
  3. *Psychological versus metaphysical certainty (Gewirth, Loeb):* Clear and distinct perceptions provide psychological certainty, an inability to doubt them while attending. God provides metaphysical certainty, the assurance that no reason for doubt remains. Loeb developed this as a theory of the *stability* of belief: knowledge of God makes our convictions immune to being shaken by later doubts.
  4. *The coherence or consistency interpretation (Frankfurt):* Descartes's aim is not to prove that clear perceptions correspond to reality. It is to show that reason is not self-undermining. The hypothesis of a deceiving God raises the possibility that reason, properly used, leads to the conclusion that it is unreliable. The proof of God shows instead that reason, properly used, leads to the conclusion that it is reliable. Truth in the relevant sense is "absolute certainty" (Second Replies, AT VII 144–145).
     * *Strength:* It explains why Descartes is untroubled by "absolute falsity."
     * *Weakness:* Descartes speaks in correspondence terms in many places, so the reading seems to weaken his ambitions.
  5. *Dialectical readings (Curley and others):* Descartes's response is addressed to the skeptic's particular grounds for doubt. The deceiving-God hypothesis is not a self-evident possibility but a belief with a content. Once the meditator sees that God is perfect and non-deceiving, the hypothesis loses its force as a reason for doubt. Recognizing this requires no general validation of reason.
* **Della Rocca's dilemma:** Michael Della Rocca has argued that the Circle reveals a deeper structural problem. Either clear and distinct perceptions confer justification independently of God, in which case God's epistemic role is redundant. Or they do not, in which case the proof of God, which relies on them, is unjustified. The dilemma presses all interpretations that try to give God a substantive epistemic role without circularity.
* **Assessment:** The texts are not fully consistent. *Meditation* III extends doubt to simple clear perceptions whenever one is not attending to them. The Second Replies seem to say that some perceptions are so simple and evident that they can never be doubted. The most defensible reconstruction distinguishes two levels.
  * Present, attentive clear perception produces assent that cannot be withheld while it lasts. This is what allows the proof of God to proceed.
  * Knowledge of God transforms such perceptions into *scientia*. They become stable across time, independent of present attention, and immune to the skeptical hypothesis of a deceiving origin.
  
  Whether this escapes circularity depends on whether a perception that cannot be doubted while attended to is thereby *justified*, or merely psychologically compelling. That question cannot be settled by textual exegesis alone. It is a substantive question about the nature of epistemic justification.
* **Philosophical significance:** The Cartesian Circle is the classic case of what William Alston called "epistemic circularity." It concerns whether one can justify the reliability of one's basic cognitive sources without using them. Contemporary virtue epistemology, notably Sosa's, has read Descartes's project as an attempt to move from "animal" knowledge, reliable first-order cognition, to "reflective" knowledge, which includes an understanding of why one's faculties are reliable. On this reading the Circle is not a logical blunder but an instance of a structural problem every comprehensive epistemology must face. Similar problems arise for empiricist justifications of induction by induction, and for naturalist attempts to justify cognition by cognitive science.

#### Philosophy of Baruch Spinoza

**The Geometrical Method**: `rationalism`
* **Central thesis:** The *Ethics* is "demonstrated in geometrical order" (*ordine geometrico demonstrata*). Each Part proceeds from definitions, axioms, and postulates through propositions with demonstrations, corollaries, and scholia, supplemented by prefaces and appendices. For Spinoza this form is not merely expository. Reality is a causal order in which everything follows necessarily from the nature of God, and adequate knowledge of an effect depends on and involves knowledge of its cause (E1a4). The proper order of philosophizing must therefore reproduce the order of being: "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things" (E2p7).
* **Historical context:** Spinoza had already used the geometrical form in his *Principles of Cartesian Philosophy* (1663). That work reconstructed Parts I–II of Descartes's *Principles* in this form, following the synthetic exposition Descartes appended to his Second Replies. Lodewijk Meyer's preface to the book warned that it did not express Spinoza's own views. In the *Ethics*, Spinoza applies the method to his own system. In doing so he reverses Descartes's preference. Descartes held that metaphysics must proceed analytically, by meditation that liberates the mind from sensory prejudice. Spinoza begins synthetically from the nature of God, the first in the order of being.
* **Reasons for the method:**
  * *Order of being:* Philosophers have "not observed the order of philosophizing." They believed that the divine nature, which should be contemplated first because it is prior in knowledge and in nature, comes last. They also thought the objects of the senses come first (E2p10s). The *Ethics* corrects this by beginning with God.
  * *Mathematics as a norm of truth free of final causes:* "Truth would have been hidden from the human race to eternity" had not mathematics, "which is concerned not with ends but only with the essences and properties of figures," shown another standard of truth (E1 Appendix). Geometry is the model of explanation through essences and causes without purposes.
  * *Naturalizing the human:* The Preface to Part III announces that Spinoza will treat human actions and appetites "just as if it were a question of lines, planes, or bodies." Human beings are not "a dominion within a dominion" (*imperium in imperio*), exempt from the laws of nature. The geometrical method expresses the thesis that human affects follow from the same necessity as everything else.
* **The method of the Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect:** The unfinished TIE explains why method need not precede knowledge.
  * *The ethical motive:* The work opens with a quest for the "true good." Wealth, honor, and sensual pleasure are uncertain goods that distract the mind. The highest good is "knowledge of the union that the mind has with the whole of Nature" (§13).
  * *Four modes of perception (§19):* perception from hearsay or signs; from "vague experience"; perception in which the essence of a thing is inferred from another thing, but not adequately; and perception of a thing through its essence alone or through knowledge of its proximate cause. Only the last is fully adequate. The *Ethics* later reorganizes this list into three kinds of knowledge.
  * *No regress of method:* One might think a method for finding truth would require a further method for finding it, and so on. Spinoza replies that this is like supposing one needs a hammer to forge a hammer, and another hammer to forge that, without end. Humans first made simple tools from natural materials, then used them to make better ones (§§30–31). Similarly, "we have a true idea" (*habemus enim ideam veram*, §33). Method is not a precondition of knowledge but "reflexive knowledge, or the idea of an idea" (§38). The best method shows how the mind is to be directed according to the standard of a given true idea. Truth needs no external sign: whoever has a true idea knows at the same time that he has it (§§35–36; compare E2p43).
* **Genetic definition:** The TIE requires a definition of a created thing to include its proximate cause, so that all its properties can be deduced from it (§§95–96). A circle should be defined not as a figure all of whose points are equidistant from the center, which is a property, but as the figure described by a line with one end fixed and the other moving. A definition of an uncreated thing must exclude every cause. The thing must need nothing besides its own being to be explained (§97). A letter to Simon de Vries (Ep. 9, 1663) distinguishes two types of definition. One explains a thing as it exists outside the intellect, and must therefore be true. The other explains a thing as we conceive or can conceive it, and need only be conceivable. Spinoza's definitions in *Ethics* I have the verbal form of stipulations ("By substance I understand…"). Their function, however, is to state the essences or genetic causes of what is defined.
* **Interpretive debate:**
  * *Literary form:* Harry Wolfson argued that the geometrical method is a literary form. The real argument lies "behind" it, in a dense network of allusions to medieval Jewish, Arabic, and scholastic philosophy, which must be reconstructed to understand the text.
  * *Constitutive form:* Edwin Curley, Don Garrett, and others argue that the method is philosophically constitutive. The geometrical order expresses Spinoza's conviction that explanation proceeds from essences through necessary connections. The definitions are genetic and the demonstrations trace real dependence. Curley's own reconstruction (*Behind the Geometrical Method*) nonetheless shows how much the argument depends on Spinoza's dialogue with Descartes and Hobbes.
  
  Both sides agree that the scholia, prefaces, and appendices contain a discursive argument. It diagnoses prejudices and anticipates objections, and it cannot be reduced to the demonstrative chain.
* **Does the form establish the truth of the premises?** It does not. A deductive structure guarantees validity, not soundness. The truth of the system depends on the truth of its definitions and axioms. Spinoza holds that these are self-evident to an attentive intellect and that genetic definitions are true by construction. Critics have shown, however, that many demonstrations contain gaps, unstated premises, or equivocations. The best known are E1p5 and the transition to conatus in E3p4–6. Jonathan Bennett's commentary catalogues many such problems.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Kant:* The mathematical method cannot be transferred to philosophy. Mathematics constructs its concepts in intuition and can therefore define and demonstrate. Philosophy works with given concepts and can only analyze them discursively (*Critique of Pure Reason*, "Discipline of Pure Reason").
  * *Hegel:* Spinoza's method, beginning from definitions, presupposes its starting points instead of deriving them. It is a form of "external reflection" that cannot grasp the self-development of the concept. Hegel ties this methodological defect to the substantive one that Spinoza's substance is not also subject.
  * *Spinoza's likely reply:* His starting points are not arbitrary posits. They are adequate ideas whose truth is internally evident and whose fruitfulness is shown by what follows from them, much as a true idea of a circle shows its truth in generating its properties.
* **Philosophical significance:** The *Ethics* is the most ambitious attempt in the history of philosophy to present an entire metaphysics, epistemology, psychology, and ethics as a single deductive system. Its method raises lasting questions:
  * How philosophical explanation relates to mathematical demonstration.
  * Whether definitions can be "real."
  * Whether philosophy can be presuppositionless.
  * How the form of a philosophical work relates to its content.
  
  The TIE's account of method—truth as its own standard, method as reflexive knowledge—is an important alternative to Cartesian methodological doubt. Spinoza does not begin by doubting everything to find an indubitable foundation. He begins from a true idea and lets it disclose its own standard.

**Substance Monism**: `substance monism`
* **Central thesis:** There is only one substance, God, a being "absolutely infinite," consisting of infinitely many attributes, each of which expresses an eternal and infinite essence. Everything else that exists is a mode of this substance, existing in it and conceived through it. "Whatever is, is in God, and nothing can be or be conceived without God" (E1p15).
* **Key definitions and axioms of Ethics I:**
  * *E1d1 (cause of itself, causa sui):* "that whose essence involves existence, or that whose nature cannot be conceived except as existing."
  * *E1d3 (substance):* "what is in itself and is conceived through itself, that is, that whose concept does not require the concept of another thing, from which it must be formed."
  * *E1d4 (attribute):* "what the intellect perceives of a substance, as constituting its essence."
  * *E1d5 (mode):* "the affections of a substance, or that which is in another through which it is also conceived."
  * *E1d6 (God):* "a being absolutely infinite, that is, a substance consisting of an infinity of attributes, of which each one expresses an eternal and infinite essence." The explication distinguishes what is infinite only "in its own kind," of which infinite attributes can be denied, from what is absolutely infinite, to whose essence pertains whatever expresses essence and involves no negation.
  * *Axioms:* Everything that is, is either in itself or in another (E1a1). What cannot be conceived through another must be conceived through itself (E1a2). Knowledge of an effect depends on and involves knowledge of its cause (E1a4). Things that have nothing in common cannot be understood through one another (E1a5). If a thing can be conceived as not existing, its essence does not involve existence (E1a7).
* **The argument for monism (E1p1–15):**
  1. Substance is prior in nature to its affections (p1).
  2. Two substances with different attributes have nothing in common (p2).
  3. Things that have nothing in common cannot be the cause of one another (p3).
  4. Two or more distinct things are distinguished either by a difference in their attributes or by a difference in their affections (p4).
  5. In nature there cannot be two or more substances of the same nature or attribute (p5).
  6. One substance cannot be produced by another substance (p6).
  7. It pertains to the nature of a substance to exist (p7).
  8. Every substance is necessarily infinite (p8).
  9. The more reality or being a thing has, the more attributes belong to it (p9). Each attribute must be conceived through itself (p10).
  10. God, a substance consisting of infinite attributes, necessarily exists (p11).
  11. No attribute of a substance can be truly conceived from which it follows that substance can be divided (p12). A substance that is absolutely infinite is indivisible (p13).
  12. Except God, no substance can be or be conceived (p14).
  13. Whatever is, is in God (p15).
* **Analysis of the critical steps:**
  * *E1p5 and Leibniz's objection:* The demonstration runs as follows. If there were two substances of the same attribute, they would have to be distinguished either by attributes or by affections. They cannot differ by attributes, since they are supposed to share one. They cannot differ by affections either. Substance is prior to its affections, so if the affections are set aside and substance is considered in itself, nothing remains to distinguish them. Leibniz objected that two substances might share one attribute and differ in another. A could have attributes *a* and *b*, and B attributes *b* and *c*. The proof seems to exclude only substances that share *all* their attributes.
  * *Defenses of E1p5:* Each attribute, being conceived through itself and expressing the essence of substance, constitutes the whole essence of its substance (E1d4, p10). If *b* constitutes the essence of both A and B, they would have the same essence and could not be distinguished. Michael Della Rocca argues that Spinoza's principle of sufficient reason supports E1p5. It implies a version of the identity of indiscernibles: there would be no reason for two substances' distinctness, so there cannot be two. Whether these defenses succeed depends on whether an attribute's "constituting the essence" excludes its being shared.
  * *E1p11, the existence of God:* Spinoza gives several proofs.
    * *Ontological:* God is a substance, and existence pertains to the nature of substance (p7).
    * *From the PSR:* For each thing a reason must be assigned for its existence or non-existence. Nothing could prevent God from existing, so God exists.
    * *A posteriori:* The ability to exist is power. If only finite beings existed, they would be more powerful than an absolutely infinite being, which is absurd.
    
    All these proofs depend on the coherence of the concept of an absolutely infinite substance. They face the objections that confronted Descartes's ontological argument.
  * *E1p14:* Since God exists (p11) and possesses all attributes (d6), any other substance would have to share an attribute with God, contradicting p5. This step depends on two further claims. One substance can have more than one attribute, against Descartes's view that each substance has one principal attribute. God has all attributes, so there are none left for another substance. Spinoza defends the first claim at E1p10s. Nothing is clearer than that each being must be conceived under some attribute, and that the more reality it has, the more attributes it has.
* **Indivisibility and extension (E1p15s; Ep. 12):** Opponents argued that extended substance is divisible, finite, and passive, and therefore unworthy of God. Spinoza replies that extension *as substance* is indivisible. Division and parts belong only to the modes of extension. Quantity can be conceived in two ways: abstractly or superficially by the imagination, which finds it divisible and finite; or as substance by the intellect, which finds it infinite, unique, and indivisible. His illustration is water. Insofar as it is water, we conceive it as divided and its parts as separate. Insofar as it is corporeal substance, it is neither separated nor divisible. The letter to Meyer on the infinite (Ep. 12) diagnoses the paradoxes about infinite quantity as arising from confusing measure, time, and number, which are aids of the imagination, with the infinite nature of substance.
* **The Cartesian background:** Descartes defined substance as "a thing that exists in such a way as to depend on no other thing for its existence." He admitted that only God strictly satisfies this definition. Created substances, minds and bodies, are substances only in a secondary sense: they need only God's concurrence (*Principles* I.51–52). Spinoza removes the secondary sense. If substance is what is in itself and conceived through itself, only God is substance. Descartes also inferred distinct substances from a real distinction between thought and extension. Spinoza denies that inference. Attributes can be really distinct, each conceived without the other, while belonging to one substance (E1p10s).
* **Major objections:**
  * *Bayle:* In his *Historical and Critical Dictionary* (1697), Bayle argued that if all things are modes of one substance, God is the subject of contradictory predicates. God would be both the Turk and the Christian who kill each other, both suffering and causing suffering. This objection depends on reading modes as properties predicated of God. On this issue see the entry on modes.
  * *Leibniz:* The proof of monism depends on the questionable E1p5. Leibniz's own metaphysics defends a plurality of individual substances, the monads.
  * *The concept of absolute infinity:* The coherence of an absolutely infinite substance with infinitely many attributes is not self-evident. Leibniz objected that Spinoza had not shown the concept of such a substance to be possible.
* **Interpretations:**
  * *Bennett's field metaphysics:* Finite bodies are not things that move through space but regions of extension that have certain qualities, "thickenings" of space that pass from region to region.
  * *Substance as the ground of everything (Della Rocca):* Monism follows from the demand that everything be intelligible.
  * *Contemporary taxonomy:* In current terms, Spinoza holds "priority monism" about substance, in Jonathan Schaffer's sense. There is one fundamental entity on which everything depends. He is not an "existence monist" who denies that there are many things, since there are infinitely many modes.
* **Philosophical significance:** Spinoza's monism draws the most rigorous conclusion from the rationalist concept of substance. If substance is self-sufficient in being and conception, there can be only one. The argument shapes later debates about substance, individuality, and the ultimate ground of being. Its themes are taken up in German Idealism's "absolute" (Schelling, Hegel) and in contemporary debates over the priority of the whole versus the parts.

**God or Nature and Immanent Causation**: `immanentism`
* **Central thesis:** God is not a transcendent creator who stands outside the world and makes it by an act of will. God is the immanent cause of all things: "God is the immanent, not the transitive, cause of all things" (E1p18). All things are in God and follow necessarily from the divine nature. The Preface to Part IV speaks of "that eternal and infinite being we call God, or Nature" (*Deus seu Natura*). E4p4d speaks of "the power of God, or Nature." The identity is qualified by the distinction between *natura naturans*, Nature as active cause, and *natura naturata*, Nature as effect.
* **God's causation (E1p16–18, 25):**
  * "From the necessity of the divine nature there must follow infinitely many things in infinitely many modes, that is, everything that can fall under an infinite intellect" (E1p16). God is the efficient cause of all things, a cause through himself, and absolutely the first cause (E1p16c1–3).
  * God acts from the laws of his own nature alone, compelled by no one (E1p17). God alone is a free cause (E1p17c2), in the sense of E1d7: a thing is free when it exists from the necessity of its own nature alone and is determined to act by itself alone.
  * God is the efficient cause not only of the existence of things but also of their essence (E1p25).
* **Immanent versus transitive causation:** A transitive cause produces an effect outside itself. An immanent cause produces an effect that remains in it. The distinction appears in scholastic logic and in Spinoza's *Short Treatise*. In the *Ethics*, it follows from monism. If nothing exists outside God, then whatever God causes is in God. The causal relation between God and things is therefore a relation of dependence within a single reality, not a relation between a creator and an external creation.
* **Natura naturans and natura naturata (E1p29s):** *Natura naturans* is "what is in itself and is conceived through itself, or such attributes of substance as express an eternal and infinite essence, that is, God insofar as he is considered as a free cause." *Natura naturata* is "whatever follows from the necessity of God's nature, or from any of God's attributes, that is, all the modes." God is identified with Nature, but primarily with Nature as active, productive power. The identification does not make God the mere sum of finite things. Spinoza explicitly rejects this reading in a letter to Oldenburg (Ep. 73, 1675). Those who think the *Theological-Political Treatise* rests on identifying God with Nature, "by which they understand a certain mass or corporeal matter," are entirely mistaken.
* **The rejection of divine intellect and will:** Spinoza denies the traditional conception of divine agency.
  * *Equivocal names:* If intellect and will belong to God's essence, they would differ from ours entirely. They would agree with ours in name only, "as the dog, the heavenly constellation, agrees with the dog, the barking animal" (E1p17s).
  * *Intellect and will as effects:* Actual intellect, finite or infinite, together with will, desire, and love, belongs to *natura naturata* (E1p31). It is an effect of God, not his essence.
  * *Will is necessary, not free:* Will is not a free cause but a necessary one (E1p32). God does not act from freedom of will.
  * *Against divine choice of the good (E1p33s2):* Some hold that God does everything for the sake of the good. They are further from the truth than those who make everything depend on God's indifferent will. Positing an independent good "seems to subject God to fate," as if God looked to it as a model or aimed at it as a goal.
* **Causation and conception:** E1a4 states that knowledge of an effect depends on and involves knowledge of its cause. Spinoza often speaks of a "cause or reason" (*causa seu ratio*, E1p11d2). Causal dependence and conceptual dependence coincide: to be caused by something is to be conceived through it. This principle unifies the system. It explains why modes are "in" and "conceived through" God. It also underlies the attribute barrier: nothing can be conceived through an attribute other than its own, so modes of one attribute cannot cause modes of another (E2p6; see Mind and Body). Della Rocca calls this "causal rationalism." Spinoza's world is one in which causation, explanation, and conception are a single relation.
* **Traditional theism, preserved or rejected?**
  * *Rejected:* creation from nothing by an act of will; a personal God with intellect and will analogous to ours; providence as purposive governance; miracles as violations of natural order; anthropomorphic attributes such as love, anger, and mercy toward individuals (E5p17c: God, strictly speaking, loves no one); God's incorporeality. God is an "extended thing" (E2p2).
  * *Preserved:* God's necessary existence, eternity, infinity, uniqueness, and status as cause of all things; God as the object of the highest knowledge and love (*Ethics* V); and much traditional theological vocabulary, deliberately redefined.
  * *Ep. 73:* Spinoza claims that his view that "all things are in God and move in God" agrees with Paul (Acts 17:28) and perhaps with all the ancient philosophers and the ancient Hebrews.
* **Pantheism or panentheism?** The term "pantheism" was coined after Spinoza's death, by John Toland in 1705.
  * *Pantheism:* God is identical with the whole of nature.
  * *Panentheism:* All things are in God but God is not exhausted by them. The term was coined later still, by Karl Krause.
  * *Curley's causal reading:* God is *natura naturans*, the attributes and their laws. Finite things are effects of God, not parts or properties of him. This reading makes Spinoza closer to panentheism, though without any transcendence of God beyond nature.
  * *Hegel:* Spinoza's position is not atheism but "acosmism." The world, not God, loses its reality.
  * *Jacobi:* In the Pantheism Controversy of 1785, Jacobi argued that Spinozism was atheism and fatalism, and that it is the consistent outcome of all rationalist philosophy.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Theists:* A God without intellect, will, or love for individuals is not God in any religiously relevant sense. Spinoza's use of the name is equivocal.
  * *Naturalists:* If God is Nature, the word "God" is superfluous. Spinoza's reply is implicit in the system. Nature, properly understood, has the properties traditionally reserved for God: necessary existence, infinity, self-causation, and the status of first cause of everything. It is also the proper object of the intellectual love that constitutes blessedness.
  * *Internal:* If God acts from necessity, it is unclear in what sense God is "free." Spinoza's answer is that God's freedom is self-determination. It is the absence of external constraint, not indifference.
* **Philosophical significance:** Spinoza's immanent God is the most influential alternative within the Western tradition to the transcendent creator God. It shaped the "religion of nature" of German Romanticism; Novalis called Spinoza "God-intoxicated." It influenced the philosophy of the absolute in Schelling and Hegel. It also shaped later scientific naturalisms that use religious language for the order of nature. Einstein, asked whether he believed in God, said he believed in "Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of all that exists, but not in a God who concerns himself with the fate and actions of human beings." Spinoza's identification of causation with conceptual dependence raises a central question for later rationalism and for contemporary metaphysics of grounding: does explanation track a single relation of dependence, or many?

**Attributes**
* **Central thesis:** An attribute is "what the intellect perceives of a substance, as constituting its essence" (E1d4). God has infinitely many attributes, each of which expresses God's eternal and infinite essence and is conceived through itself (E1p10). We know only two of them: Thought and Extension. God is both a thinking thing and an extended thing (E2p1–2). The attributes are really distinct, since none can be conceived through another. They are not numerically distinct, since they belong to one substance.
* **Key concepts:**
  * *Expression (exprimere):* Each attribute expresses God's essence completely, though in its own way. Gilles Deleuze made "expression" the key to Spinoza's ontology.
  * *Conceptual independence:* Each attribute is conceived through itself (E1p10). Modes of one attribute involve the concept of their own attribute and not of any other (E2p6). This "attribute barrier" forbids causal or explanatory relations between attributes.
  * *What we know:* "We neither feel nor perceive any singular things except bodies and modes of thinking" (E2a5).
* **The first interpretive problem—subjective or objective?** Do attributes belong to substance as it is in itself, or are they ways in which an intellect conceives it?
  * *Subjectivist reading (Wolfson, and in different terms Hegel):* Attributes are aspects under which the intellect perceives an essence that in itself is simple. This follows the medieval Jewish and Arabic view that divine attributes are human ways of conceiving God's unity. Textual support:
    * The word "perceives" in E1d4.
    * Letter 9: "By substance I understand what is in itself and conceived through itself. … By attribute I understand the same, except that it is called attribute in relation to the intellect, which attributes such and such a nature to substance." Spinoza illustrates with a single surface called "plane" insofar as it reflects all rays without change, and "white" insofar as it is related to someone looking at it. He also uses the names "Israel" and "Jacob" for the same patriarch.
  * *Objectivist reading (Gueroult and most current interpreters):* Attributes are real features of substance.
    * The intellect perceives *truly*, so what it perceives as constituting the essence does constitute it.
    * The intellect is itself a mode of the attribute of Thought. If attributes existed only for an intellect, Extension would depend on a mode of Thought. This contradicts the conceptual independence of attributes and makes Thought privileged.
    * E1p10s says the attributes are "really distinct."
  * *Intermediate readings:* Some recent interpreters argue that the dichotomy is false. Attributes are both real and essentially related to an intellect's perception, because the essence of substance includes its being intelligible.
* **The second interpretive problem—real but not numerical distinction:** If each attribute constitutes the essence of substance, does God have infinitely many essences? Spinoza denies it. The attributes "express" one essence.
  * *Della Rocca's referential-opacity reading:* Attributes are distinct ways of conceiving the same thing. Contexts such as "x is conceived under the attribute of thought" are intensional. The same thing can be conceived under different attributes without contradiction. Explanatory relations hold only within an attribute, because explanation is attribute-relative.
  * *Bennett:* There are "trans-attribute modes." Attributes function like differentiae of an underlying structure. This reading has been criticized for introducing something underlying the attributes, which Spinoza does not mention.
  
  What is at stake is whether Spinoza's monism is coherent, and how mind and body can be "one and the same thing" (E2p7s).
* **The third interpretive problem—infinitely many attributes:**
  * *Bennett's reading:* "Infinite" in E1d6 may mean simply "all," so God has all attributes and there may be only two. Most interpreters reject this. The explication of E1d6 contrasts the absolutely infinite with what is infinite in its own kind, which suggests a genuine infinity of attributes.
  * *Tschirnhaus's question:* Through Schuller, Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus asked why each thing is expressed in infinitely many attributes, yet the human mind knows only two (Ep. 63, 65). Spinoza replies that the human mind is the idea of the human body, which involves no attribute except Extension. Each thing is expressed in infinitely many ways in God's infinite intellect. But the ideas that constitute the "minds" of the modes of other attributes neither know one another nor know the modes of Extension (Ep. 64, 66).
  * *The privilege of Thought:* Tschirnhaus's question exposes an asymmetry. For each mode of every attribute there must be an idea in God's infinite intellect. So the attribute of Thought must contain ideas of modes of all attributes. Thought seems to have infinitely more modes than Extension, which threatens the equality of the attributes. Yitzhak Melamed has argued that Spinoza accepts this structure. Ideas have a "multifaceted" structure: each idea has infinitely many facets, one corresponding to each attribute. The doctrine of the parallelism of attributes is therefore more complex than the identity of mind and body.
* **Relation to Descartes:** Descartes held that each substance has a principal attribute: thought for minds, extension for bodies. Their real distinction implies distinct substances. Spinoza accepts the real distinction of attributes but denies that it implies distinct substances. One substance can be conceived completely under each of its attributes. This transforms Cartesian dualism into a monism of substance with a dualism, or rather a pluralism, of attributes.
* **Major objections:**
  * The notion of really distinct attributes constituting one essence may be incoherent, or it may conceal a dualism of essences.
  * Since we know only two attributes, the claim that there are infinitely many is either unverifiable or derived from the concept of an absolutely infinite being, which itself needs justification.
  * The privilege of Thought threatens the parallelism the system needs.
* **Philosophical significance:** Spinoza's theory of attributes is the historical origin of "dual-aspect" or "double-aspect" monism, the view that the mental and the physical are two aspects of one underlying reality. Versions of this view appear in nineteenth-century psychophysics (Fechner), in neutral monism, and in contemporary philosophy of mind. Any genealogy must respect the differences. Spinoza's attributes are not properties of individual things but essence-constituting features of substance. His "aspects" are not perspectives of observers. The theory raises enduring questions about the relation between conceptual and metaphysical distinctness, and about whether the same entity can be fully grasped under irreducibly different descriptions.

**Modes and the Structure of Nature**
* **Central thesis:** Everything other than God and his attributes is a mode: an affection of substance, in God and conceived through God (E1d5, p15). Modes form a structured hierarchy. Infinite modes follow immediately or mediately from the absolute nature of the attributes. Finite modes, singular things, follow through an infinite chain of finite causes. Every finite thing is thus the effect of two kinds of causation. It depends on God's nature as expressed in its attribute and its infinite modes. It is also determined by other finite things.
* **Infinite modes (E1p21–23):** "All things that follow from the absolute nature of any of God's attributes have always had to exist and be infinite, or are, through the same attribute, eternal and infinite" (E1p21). These are the immediate infinite modes. Things that follow from an attribute insofar as it is modified by an immediate infinite mode must also be infinite (E1p22). These are the mediate infinite modes. Spinoza names examples only in a letter to Schuller (Ep. 64).
  * In Thought, the immediate infinite mode is the "absolutely infinite intellect."
  * In Extension, it is "motion and rest."
  * The mediate infinite mode of Extension is "the face of the whole universe" (*facies totius universi*). It "although varying in infinite ways, yet remains always the same." Here Spinoza refers to the scholium after Lemma 7 of the "physical digression" in Part II, where the whole of nature is conceived as one individual whose parts vary without any change in the whole.
* **The role of the infinite modes:** They mediate between the eternal infinite substance and the finite, changing things. Curley interpreted them as the general laws of nature, or the facts expressed by those laws. Finite modes then correspond to particular facts. The infinite modes are the eternal, invariant structure of each attribute, and particular things are determined within that structure. Spinoza's sparse remarks leave the doctrine underdetermined.
* **Finite modes and their causation (E1p28):** "Every singular thing, or any thing which is finite and has a determinate existence, can neither exist nor be determined to produce an effect unless it is determined to exist and produce an effect by another cause, which is also finite and has a determinate existence; and again, this cause also can neither exist nor be determined to produce an effect unless it is determined … by another, which is also finite … and so on, to infinity." The demonstration explains that whatever is determined to exist and act is determined by God (E1p26). But what is finite cannot follow from God's absolute nature, which produces only what is infinite (E1p21). It must follow from God insofar as he is modified by a finite mode.
  * *Two axes of causation:* There is a "vertical" relation of dependence on God through the attributes and infinite modes, and a "horizontal" relation of determination by other finite modes. The horizontal chain is not an alternative to divine causation. It is divine causation, since each finite cause is God insofar as he is affected by that mode.
* **Deriving the finite from the infinite:** If only infinite things follow from God's absolute nature, how do finite things arise? E1p28 seems to answer that finite things follow from other finite things to infinity. This explains each finite thing by others, but leaves unexplained why there are finite things at all. Readings of the problem differ.
  * *Hegel and Jacobi:* Spinoza cannot derive the finite from the infinite. The finite is either illusory, whence Hegel's charge of acosmism, or unexplained.
  * *Garrett and others:* The whole infinite series of finite modes follows from God's nature, though not from God's absolute nature alone. The series is necessitated by the infinite modes together with the infinite chain.
  * *Spinoza's late correspondence:* Tschirnhaus asked how the variety of bodies can be deduced a priori from extension. Spinoza replied that from extension as Descartes conceives it—an inert mass—the existence of bodies cannot be demonstrated. Matter must be explained through an attribute that expresses an eternal and infinite essence (Ep. 80–83). He admits that he had not yet set this out in proper order.
* **What it is to be "in" God—inherence or causation?** E1a1 and E1d5 describe modes as "in another" (*in alio*). There are three main interpretations.
  * *Causal dependence (Curley):* For modes to be "in" God is for them to be causally dependent on God, as particular facts depend on general laws. Modes are not properties of God. This avoids Bayle's objection that God would be the subject of contradictory predicates: both Turk and Christian, both killer and killed.
  * *Inherence and predication (Melamed and others):* Spinoza's language of "affections" (*affectiones*) and "in" is the traditional language of inherence. Modes are genuinely properties of substance. Bayle's objection is answered by the qualifier "insofar as" (*quatenus*). Contradictory predicates hold of God not simpliciter but insofar as God is affected in different modes. On this reading modes are both in God, as properties, and caused by God.
  * *Unity of the relations (Della Rocca):* Driven by the PSR, inherence, causation, and conception are one relation. To be in God, to be caused by God, and to be conceived through God are the same.
* **Formal essence, duration, and eternity:** The ideas of singular things that do not exist "must be comprehended in God's infinite idea in the same way as the formal essences of the singular things … are contained in God's attributes" (E2p8). Finite things therefore have two modes of being.
  * *Essence:* They exist eternally as essences contained in the attributes.
  * *Actual existence:* They exist in duration when determined by finite causes.
  
  This distinction prepares the doctrine of the eternity of the mind in *Ethics* V.
* **Individuals:** A finite thing is not a self-standing substance. Yet it has its own determinate essence and, by E3p6–7, a striving (conatus) that constitutes its actual essence. The "physical digression" after E2p13 defines a composite individual as a set of bodies communicating their motions in a fixed ratio. An individual retains its nature as long as that ratio is preserved, even when its parts are replaced. Individuality is therefore a matter of degree and of structural stability.
* **Major objections:**
  * The derivation of finitude from infinity is incomplete.
  * The doctrine of infinite modes is obscure. Its few examples, especially the "face of the whole universe," are underdetermined.
  * If finite things are modes of God, it is not clear that they are genuine individuals rather than merely transitory configurations of the one substance. This is Hegel's "acosmism" charge. Spinoza's answer lies in conatus: each finite thing is a determinate expression of God's power, and its power is its essence.
* **Philosophical significance:** Spinoza's account of modes poses the central question of every substance monism: how the one relates to the many, and the infinite to the finite. The distinction between infinite modes and the infinite chain of finite causes is an early attempt to articulate the relation between laws of nature and particular events. It anticipates questions in the philosophy of science about the necessity of laws and the dependence of particular facts on general ones. The debate over whether modes inhere in or are caused by substance remains central to Spinoza scholarship and to contemporary theories of grounding and dependence.

**Necessitarianism and Determinism**: `necessitarianism`, `determinism`
* **Central thesis:** Everything that exists, exists necessarily, and nothing could have been otherwise. "In nature there is nothing contingent, but all things have been determined from the necessity of the divine nature to exist and produce an effect in a certain way" (E1p29). "Things could have been produced by God in no other way, and in no other order than they have been produced" (E1p33). Contingency is not a feature of things but a defect of our knowledge. Human actions are no exception. There is no free will.
* **The principle of sufficient reason:** "For each thing there must be assigned a cause, or reason, as much for its existence as for its non-existence" (E1p11d2). Spinoza's rationalism is the thesis that everything is intelligible: there are no brute facts. E1a3 adds: "From a given determinate cause the effect follows necessarily; and conversely, if there is no determinate cause, it is impossible for an effect to follow." Della Rocca argues that the PSR is the master principle of Spinoza's system. Monism, necessitarianism, the identity of causation and conception, and the rejection of free will all follow from consistently refusing to admit anything unexplained.
* **The argument (E1p33d):**
  1. All things follow necessarily from God's nature (E1p16) and are determined to exist and act in a certain way by that necessity (E1p29).
  2. Suppose things could have been of another nature or determined to act in another way.
  3. Then God's nature could also have been other than it is.
  4. That other nature would have to exist as well, since it pertains to God's nature to exist (E1p11).
  5. There would then be two or more Gods, which is absurd (E1p14c1).
  6. Hence things could not have been produced in any other way or order.
* **Contingency as ignorance (E1p33s1; E4d3–4):**
  * *Necessity:* A thing is necessary either by reason of its essence, if its essence involves existence, or by reason of its cause.
  * *Contingency:* A thing is called contingent only "because of a defect of our knowledge." We do not know that its essence involves a contradiction, or we do not know the order of its causes.
  * *Epistemic definitions:* In Part IV, singular things are called "contingent" insofar as, attending only to their essence, we find nothing that necessarily posits or excludes their existence (E4d3). They are called "possible" insofar as, attending to their causes, we do not know whether those causes are determined to produce them (E4d4). These are epistemic notions, useful in practical life, without metaphysical correlates.
  * *The view of reason:* "It is of the nature of reason to regard things not as contingent but as necessary" (E2p44). Reason perceives things "under a certain species of eternity" (E2p44c2).
* **Strict or moderate necessitarianism?**
  * *Strict reading (Garrett):* Spinoza holds that the actual world is the only possible world. Every truth, including truths about finite modes, is necessary. Finite modes derive their necessity from the infinite chain of finite causes together with God's nature. E1p33 is decisive: things could not have been produced in any other order.
  * *Moderate reading (Curley and Walski):* Only God, the attributes, and the infinite modes follow from the absolute nature of God. Finite modes are necessitated by prior finite modes (E1p28). The infinite series as a whole might therefore not be necessary in the strongest sense. This reading draws on E1p21–28 and on the claim that the finite does not follow from God's absolute nature.
  
  Much turns on E1p16 ("everything that can fall under an infinite intellect"). If it means that everything possible is actual, a principle of plenitude, then strict necessitarianism follows.
* **Human action and free will:**
  * *The will is not free:* "In the mind there is no absolute, or free, will. The mind is determined to will this or that by a cause which is also determined by another, and this again by another, and so to infinity" (E2p48).
  * *The illusion of free will:* People think themselves free because they are conscious of their volitions and appetites but ignorant of the causes by which they are determined (E1 Appendix; E2p35s; E3p2s). In Spinoza's letter to Schuller (Ep. 58), a stone set in motion by an external cause would, if it were conscious and striving to continue moving, believe itself free and think it continues of its own will. The infant believes it freely wants milk, the angry boy that he freely wants revenge, the drunk that he speaks from a free decision.
* **Necessitarianism and responsibility:** Willem van Blijenbergh objected in an exchange of letters (Ep. 18–24, 27). If everything is necessary, sin is excused, and the distinction between good and wicked people disappears. Spinoza's later reply to Oldenburg (Ep. 78, 1676) is that the wicked are not less to be feared or less harmful for being necessarily wicked. "He who goes mad from the bite of a dog is to be excused, yet he is rightly suffocated." Moral and legal responses are justified by their effects, not by desert grounded in free will. This anticipates later consequentialist and forward-looking accounts of responsibility.
* **Major objections:**
  * *Leibniz:* Leibniz distinguished absolute (logical, metaphysical) necessity from hypothetical necessity. The actual world is necessary only given God's choice of the best, while other worlds are possible in themselves. He also argued that unrealized possibilities exist, such as the events in novels, and that Spinoza's view destroys the distinction between the actual and the possible and makes divine choice impossible. Spinoza's position rejects all such unrealized possibilities as mere fictions of the imagination.
  * *Modal intuitions:* It seems obvious that things could have been otherwise. For Spinoza, this intuition arises from our ignorance of causes. The consistency of an alternative scenario with our concepts shows only the inadequacy of those concepts.
  * *The modal collapse argument:* Peter van Inwagen and others have argued that the PSR entails necessitarianism. Since necessitarianism is false, the PSR must be rejected. Spinoza accepts the entailment and draws the opposite conclusion. The contemporary debate about the PSR (Della Rocca and others) revisits exactly this choice.
* **Philosophical significance:** Spinoza is the most consistent necessitarian in the history of Western philosophy. His position remains the reference point for debates about the PSR, modal collapse, determinism and responsibility, and the relation between explanation and necessity. His therapeutic claim—that understanding the necessity of things frees us from emotional bondage—gives necessitarianism a practical significance that is developed in *Ethics* V. Recognizing that events could not have been otherwise reduces regret, hatred, and blame.

**Criticism of Teleology**: `naturalism`
* **Central thesis:** Nature does not act for ends. "All final causes are nothing but human fictions" (E1 Appendix). The belief that nature, or God, acts for purposes arises from human ignorance of causes combined with consciousness of our own appetites. It inverts the true order of explanation. It also undermines God's perfection. Value concepts derived from teleology—good, evil, order, beauty, perfection—are modes of imagining relative to human affects. They are not properties of things in themselves.
* **The genealogy of the prejudice (E1 Appendix):** Spinoza begins from two facts. All people are born ignorant of the causes of things. And all want to seek their own advantage and are conscious of this appetite. Two consequences follow.
  * People think themselves free, because they are conscious of their volitions but ignorant of their causes.
  * People always act for an end, namely their advantage, so they seek only the final causes of things.
  
  Finding in nature many things useful to them—eyes for seeing, teeth for chewing, plants and animals for food, the sun for light, the sea for fish—they infer that these were made for their use by some ruler or rulers of nature. Since they know nothing of such rulers' minds, they judge them by their own. They conclude that the gods direct everything for human use so as to bind humans to them and be honored by them. This becomes superstition. When nature harms them through storms, earthquakes, and diseases, they say the gods are angry. When the experience that such harms befall the pious and impious alike contradicts this, they say that God's judgments surpass human understanding.
* **The "sanctuary of ignorance":** Teleological explanation evades inquiry. Suppose a stone falls from a roof onto someone's head and kills him. The teleologist will argue that it fell to kill him, since such a convergence could not happen by chance. If one explains that the wind was blowing, they ask why the wind was blowing at that moment and why the man was walking by at that time. They continue asking until one takes refuge in the will of God, "that is, the sanctuary of ignorance" (*asylum ignorantiae*). Appeal to divine purpose ends inquiry where natural explanation should continue.
* **Why teleology is false:** Spinoza gives three arguments.
  1. *It inverts nature:* It makes what is a cause into an effect and what is prior into what is posterior. By E1p21–23, what is produced immediately by God is most perfect. The teleologist makes the most perfect thing the last and most remote, the goal for which earlier things exist.
  2. *It undermines God's perfection:* If God acts for an end, he wants something he lacks. Theologians distinguish an "end of need" from an "end of assimilation," but this does not help. Even acting for the sake of creatures implies that God lacked something before creating them.
  3. *It serves ideological interests:* Those who seek the true causes of natural phenomena and study nature as scholars, not fools, are denounced as heretics by people whom the vulgar honor as interpreters of nature and the gods. They "know that if ignorance is taken away, so is the foolish wonder" on which their authority rests.
* **Value concepts as modes of imagining:** Once people persuaded themselves that everything is made for them, they judged the most important thing in each to be what is most useful to them. They formed the notions of good and evil, order and confusion, warm and cold, beauty and ugliness, praise and blame, sin and merit. These "are only modes of imagining" (*modi imaginandi*) and indicate the constitution of the imagination, not the nature of anything. People call things well ordered when they are easy to imagine and remember. They call things beautiful or ugly according to how they affect their senses. The diversity of human judgments shows that these are relative.
* **Why God did not make all humans guided by reason:** Spinoza's answer is that God "did not lack material for creating all things, from the highest degree of perfection to the lowest." The laws of God's nature "were so ample that they sufficed for producing all things which can be conceived by an infinite intellect" (E1 Appendix; E1p16). The perfection of things is to be judged by their own nature and power, not by whether they please or offend human beings.
* **Perfection and the "model of human nature" (Preface to Ethics IV):** "Perfect" originally meant "completed" relative to a maker's intention. A house is perfect when finished according to the builder's plan. By forming universal ideas and models, people extended this notion to natural things, as if nature had models in view. But nature does not act for an end. The "final cause" of a house, habitation, is "nothing but a singular appetite": a human desire considered as the efficient cause of building. "Good" and "bad" indicate nothing positive in things. They are modes of thinking formed by comparison. Yet Spinoza retains these words for ethical purposes. Since we desire to form an idea of human nature as a model (*exemplar*) to look to, he defines "good" as what we certainly know to be a means of approaching that model, and "bad" as what prevents us from doing so (E4 Preface; E4d1–2). The critique of teleology thus does not abolish ethics. It reconstructs ethical concepts as relative to human nature and its power.
* **Relation to Descartes:** Descartes also excluded final causes from physics (*Meditation* IV; *Principles* I.28). His reason was that we cannot know God's purposes, which presupposes that God has them. Spinoza's critique is more radical. God has no purposes at all, and the very notion of divine purpose is an anthropomorphic projection.
* **How far does the anti-teleology extend?**
  * *Bennett's reading:* Spinoza rejects all teleological explanation, even of human action. Explanations in terms of goals must be reduced to efficient causation by present desires.
  * *Curley, Garrett, Lin:* Spinoza rejects cosmic and divine purposes but allows human teleology. Teleological explanation is a kind of efficient causation in which the idea of an end, an appetite, is the cause. This fits his own analysis of the "final cause" of a house as a desire. The doctrine of conatus, in which each thing strives to persevere in its being, seems to give all things a goal-directed structure. Garrett argued that Spinoza accepts a form of teleology implicit in conatus, though not one that involves representation or purposes external to the thing.
  
  The issue matters for whether Spinoza's psychology and ethics, which are full of goal-directed concepts, are consistent with his metaphysics.
* **Major objections:**
  * Biology seems to require functional explanations: the eye *is* for seeing.
  * Spinoza's relativization of value concepts seems to make his own ethics arbitrary.
  * The genealogy of teleology explains the origin of a belief, not its falsity, which risks a genetic fallacy.
  
  Replies are available to each. Modern accounts of biological function, such as etiological and selected-effect theories, reconstruct functional talk within efficient causation, as Spinoza's approach requires. His ethics is not arbitrary, because it is relative to an objective "model of human nature" grounded in the conatus of human beings. And the genealogy is supplemented by direct arguments against teleology.
* **Philosophical significance:** The Appendix to Part I is one of the founding texts of the critique of religion as projection, developed by Feuerbach, Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud. It is also a founding text of the critique of ideology, as in Althusser's reading. Its argument that appeals to divine will are a "sanctuary of ignorance" anticipates criticisms of "God-of-the-gaps" reasoning. Its thesis that value concepts are relative to the constitution of the evaluator shaped later naturalistic ethics. Spinoza's own ethics shows that this relativization need not lead to nihilism. Values can be relative to human nature without being merely subjective.
