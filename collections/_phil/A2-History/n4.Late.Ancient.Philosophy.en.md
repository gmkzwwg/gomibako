## Late Ancient Philosophy and Patristic Thought (c. 200–750 CE)

### Galen ☆ (129–c. 216 CE)

**Position:** Roman Imperial Philosophy · Ancient Medicine · Aristotelian and Platonic Psychology · Logic · Philosophy of Science

* **Name:** Galen of Pergamum (Ancient Greek: Γαληνός, *Galēnós*; /ˈɡeɪlən/).
* **Life:** Born in Pergamum, Roman Asia Minor; practiced medicine in Rome and served several Roman emperors.
* **Contributions:** One of antiquity's most influential physicians and scientific thinkers. Integrated empirical investigation, logical demonstration, causal explanation, and philosophical theories of the soul into medical research.
* **Major Works:** *On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato*, *On the Natural Faculties*, *On the Usefulness of the Parts*, *Introduction to Logic*, *That the Faculties of the Soul Follow the Mixtures of the Body*.

**Logic and Scientific Demonstration**
* Demonstration (*Apodeixis*), Syllogistic Reasoning, Scientific Evidence, Definition.
* What distinguishes demonstrative knowledge from probable opinion? How should logic guide scientific investigation?
* *Introduction to Logic*; fragments of *On Demonstration*.

**Causation and Medical Explanation**
* Natural Faculties, Teleology, Physical Causes, Anatomical Functions.
* How can bodily functions be explained through observation and philosophical reasoning?
* *On the Natural Faculties*, *On the Usefulness of the Parts*.

**Psychology and the Mind–Body Relationship**
* Tripartite Soul, Bodily Constitution, Rational and Non-Rational Faculties, Emotion.
* How are psychological capacities related to the physical organization of the body?
* *On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato*, *That the Faculties of the Soul Follow the Mixtures of the Body*.

**Philosophical Method and Medical Knowledge**
* Empirical Observation, Rational Explanation, Criticism of Dogmatism.
* How should competing theories be evaluated when both philosophical arguments and observational evidence are available?

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Plato, Aristotle, Stoicism, Medieval Medicine, Philosophy of Science, Historical Mind–Body Theories.

### Clement of Alexandria ☆ (c. 150–215 CE)

**Position:** Early Christian Philosophy · Alexandrian Christianity · Christian Platonism · Philosophical Theology

* **Name:** Titus Flavius Clemens, commonly known as Clement of Alexandria (Ancient Greek: Κλήμης ὁ Ἀλεξανδρεύς).
* **Life:** Early Christian philosopher and teacher associated with Alexandria, Roman Egypt; exact birthplace uncertain.
* **Contributions:** Developed an influential synthesis of Christian teaching, Greek philosophical education, ethics, and scriptural interpretation.
* **Major Works:** *Protrepticus (Exhortation to the Greeks)*, *Paedagogus (The Instructor)*, *Stromata (Miscellanies)*.

**Faith, Reason, and Philosophy**
* Faith (*Pistis*), Knowledge (*Gnōsis*), Philosophical Preparation, Christian Wisdom.
* Can Greek philosophy contribute to the understanding of Christian truth?
* *Stromata*.

**Christian Gnosis and Moral Perfection**
* Knowledge, Virtue, Spiritual Development, Moral Education.
* What distinguishes genuine knowledge from intellectual speculation or religious belief alone?

**Ethics and the Philosophical Life**
* Self-Control, Passions, Rational Discipline, the Ideal Christian Sage.
* How can Greek ethical ideals be transformed within a Christian conception of the good life?
* *Paedagogus*, *Stromata*.

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Plato, Stoicism, Origen, Alexandrian Christian Philosophy.

### Tertullian (c. 155–220 CE)

**Position:** Early Latin Christian Thought · Apologetics · Philosophical Theology

* **Name:** Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus, commonly known as Tertullian (/tərˈtʌliən/).
* **Life:** Born in Carthage, Roman North Africa; Latin Christian writer and theologian.
* **Contributions:** An early architect of Latin Christian theological vocabulary and a significant critic of the relationship between pagan philosophy and Christian doctrine.
* **Major Works:** *Apologeticus*, *De Anima (On the Soul)*, *De Praescriptione Haereticorum*, *Adversus Praxean*.

**Faith, Philosophy, and Authority**
* Faith, Reason, Revelation, Philosophical Inquiry.
* What authority should philosophical argument possess in matters of religious belief?

**Soul, Body, and Materiality**
* Corporeality of the Soul, Human Nature, Moral Responsibility.
* Can the soul be understood as a kind of body?
* *De Anima*.

**Philosophical and Theological Terminology**
* Substance (*Substantia*), Person (*Persona*), Trinitarian Language.
* How can philosophical concepts be adapted to articulate Christian doctrines?

### Alexander of Aphrodisias ☆ (fl. c. 200 CE)

**Position:** Peripatetic Philosophy · Aristotelian Commentary · Metaphysics · Philosophy of Mind · Free Will

* **Name:** Alexander of Aphrodisias (Ancient Greek: Ἀλέξανδρος ὁ Ἀφροδισιεύς, *Aléxandros ho Aphrodisieús*).
* **Life:** Philosopher associated with Aphrodisias in Caria, Roman Asia Minor; held a philosophical teaching position at Athens.
* **Contributions:** The leading ancient commentator on Aristotle, traditionally known as "the Commentator." Developed influential interpretations of Aristotelian logic, metaphysics, psychology, causation, and moral responsibility.
* **Major Works:** *On Fate*, *On the Soul (De Anima)*, *On Mixture*, *Ethical Problems*, *Problems and Solutions*, commentaries on Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, *Prior Analytics*, *Topics*, and other works.

**Aristotelian Metaphysics and Explanation**
* Substance, Form and Matter, Potentiality and Actuality, Causation.
* How can Aristotle's metaphysical doctrines be reconstructed into a coherent explanatory system?
* *Commentary on the Metaphysics*.

**Intellect and the Human Soul**
* Potential Intellect, Actual Intellect, Active Intellect, Immortality.
* What is the relationship between human thought and the separate intellect discussed in Aristotle's *De Anima* III.5?
* *On the Soul*.

**Fate, Determinism, and Free Will**
* Fate (*Heimarmenē*), Causal Determinism, Contingency, Voluntary Action.
* Can human beings act otherwise under identical prior circumstances? What distinguishes natural causation from fatalistic necessity?
* *On Fate*.

**Aristotelianism and Philosophical Commentary**
* Textual Interpretation, Logical Consistency, Philosophical Exegesis.
* Can authoritative philosophical texts be interpreted systematically without suppressing internal difficulties?

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Aristotle, Plotinus, Simplicius, Arabic Aristotelianism, Medieval Theories of Intellect.

### Origen ☆ (c. 185–253 CE)

**Position:** Early Christian Philosophy · Alexandrian Theology · Biblical Hermeneutics · Christian Platonism

* **Name:** Origen of Alexandria (Ancient Greek: Ὠριγένης, *Ōrigénēs*; /ˈɒrɪdʒən/).
* **Life:** Born in Alexandria, Roman Egypt; later established a teaching center at Caesarea. Died following imprisonment and persecution.
* **Contributions:** A foundational figure in systematic Christian philosophical theology. Developed influential accounts of God, rational creatures, free will, creation, salvation, and scriptural interpretation.
* **Major Works:** *On First Principles (Peri Archon)*, *Against Celsus*, *On Prayer*, *Commentary on John*, *Commentary on Matthew*.

**God, Logos, and Divine Reality**
* Divine Transcendence, Logos, Divine Wisdom, Trinity, Incorporeality.
* How can a transcendent God be understood as the source of an intelligible and ordered reality?
* *On First Principles* I.

**Free Will and Rational Agency**
* Free Choice, Moral Responsibility, Divine Foreknowledge, Providence.
* How can human beings possess genuine freedom within a divinely governed cosmos?
* *On First Principles* III.

**Soul, Creation, and Restoration**
* Rational Souls, Embodiment, Spiritual Development, Restoration (*Apokatastasis*).
* What is the relationship between rational souls and material existence? Can divine justice and goodness support universal restoration?
* *On First Principles* II–III.

**Philosophical Hermeneutics**
* Literal and Spiritual Interpretation, Allegory, Scriptural Meaning.
* How can a text convey multiple levels of meaning without becoming arbitrary?
* *On First Principles* IV.

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Platonism, Clement of Alexandria, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Christian Philosophical Theology.

### Plotinus ☆ (c. 204–270 CE)

**Position:** Late Ancient Platonism · Founder of Neoplatonism · Metaphysics · Philosophical Mysticism

* **Name:** Plotinus (Ancient Greek: Πλωτῖνος, *Plōtînos*; /plɒˈtaɪnəs/).
* **Life:** Probably born in Roman Egypt; studied under Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria and later established a philosophical school in Rome. Died in Campania.
* **Contributions:** Developed one of antiquity's most influential metaphysical systems, centered on the One, Intellect, and Soul. Reinterpreted Plato and Aristotle within a systematic account of reality, consciousness, causation, goodness, and philosophical ascent.
* **Major Works:** *Enneads*, comprising 54 treatises arranged into six groups of nine by Porphyry.

**The One and the First Principle**
* The One (*To Hen*), the Good (*Agathon*), Absolute Simplicity, Transcendence, Ineffability.
* Why must ultimate reality be absolutely simple? How can the One be the source of everything while remaining beyond being and thought?
* *Enneads* V.1, V.3, V.4, VI.9.

**The Three Hypostases**
* The One, Intellect (*Nous*), Soul (*Psychē*), Ontological Hierarchy.
* Why does reality consist of different levels? How do the three primary principles relate to one another?
* *Enneads* V.1, V.2.

**Procession and the Generation of Reality**
* Procession (*Prohodos*), Causation, Unity and Multiplicity, Ontological Dependence.
* How can multiplicity arise from perfect unity? Does the production of lower levels diminish the perfection of higher principles?
* *Enneads* V.2, V.4.

**Intellect, Forms, and Being**
* Intellect, Platonic Forms, Intelligible Reality, Self-Thinking Thought.
* Why are Forms understood as contents of Intellect? How are thought and being related?
* *Enneads* V.5, V.9, VI.2.

**Soul and the Structure of Consciousness**
* World Soul, Individual Souls, Discursive Reasoning, Perception, Embodiment.
* How does the soul mediate between intelligible and sensible reality? Can the soul remain connected to Intellect while animating a body?
* *Enneads* IV.3, IV.8.

**Matter, Evil, and Privation**
* Matter (*Hylē*), Privation, Evil, Ontological Deficiency.
* Is evil a positive reality or a deficiency of goodness? How can evil arise from a fundamentally good first principle?
* *Enneads* I.8, II.4.

**Beauty and the Ascent of the Soul**
* Beauty (*Kallos*), Contemplation (*Theōria*), Purification, Intelligible Beauty.
* How does beauty reveal the structure of reality? Why can aesthetic experience become a path toward metaphysical understanding?
* *Enneads* I.6, V.8.

**Virtue, Happiness, and Ethical Development**
* Virtue, Self-Purification, Happiness (*Eudaimonia*), Philosophical Life.
* What makes a person genuinely happy? How does ethical purification prepare the soul for intellectual contemplation?
* *Enneads* I.2, I.4.

**Mystical Union and the Limits of Knowledge**
* Union with the One (*Henōsis*), Negative Theology, Transcendence of Discursive Thought.
* Can ultimate reality be known conceptually? What is the relationship between intellectual understanding and non-discursive union?
* *Enneads* VI.9.

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Plato, Aristotle, Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus, Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Renaissance Platonism.

### Porphyry ☆ (c. 234–305 CE)

**Position:** Neoplatonism · Aristotelian Logic · Philosophy of Religion · Ethics

* **Name:** Porphyry of Tyre (Ancient Greek: Πορφύριος, *Porphýrios*; /ˈpɔːrfəri/).
* **Life:** Born in Tyre, Roman Phoenicia; studied with Longinus in Athens and Plotinus in Rome.
* **Contributions:** Edited Plotinus' writings into the *Enneads* and played a major role in transmitting and systematizing Neoplatonism. His logical introduction to Aristotle became foundational for medieval discussions of universals.
* **Major Works:** *Isagoge*, *Life of Plotinus*, *Sentences*, *On Abstinence from Animal Food*, *Letter to Marcella*, *Life of Pythagoras*, *Against the Christians* (fragments).

**Universals and Logical Classification**
* Genus, Species, Differentia, Property, Accident, the Problem of Universals, the Porphyrian Tree.
* Do genera and species exist independently of thought, or are they concepts derived from particular things?
* *Isagoge*.

**Aristotle and Plato**
* Logical Classification, Ontology, Philosophical Harmony.
* Can Aristotelian logic be reconciled with Platonic metaphysics?

**The Soul and Ethical Purification**
* Embodiment, Virtue, Purification, Intellectual Ascent.
* How does ethical discipline prepare the soul for higher forms of understanding?
* *Sentences*, *On Abstinence*.

**Religion and Philosophical Reason**
* Religious Practice, Theurgy, Divination, Philosophical Theology.
* Can ritual practices contribute to the soul's ascent, or is philosophical understanding sufficient?
* *Letter to Anebo* (fragmentary).

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Plotinus, Iamblichus, Boethius, Medieval Logic, the Problem of Universals.

### Iamblichus ☆ (c. 242–325 CE)

**Position:** Syrian Neoplatonism · Neopythagoreanism · Metaphysics · Theurgy

* **Name:** Iamblichus of Chalcis (Ancient Greek: Ἰάμβλιχος, *Iámblichos*).
* **Life:** Born in Chalcis in Roman Syria; studied with Porphyry and established an influential philosophical teaching tradition.
* **Contributions:** Transformed Neoplatonism through a more elaborate hierarchy of divine principles, a distinctive psychology, and a philosophical defense of theurgy.
* **Major Works:** *On the Mysteries (De Mysteriis)*, *On the Pythagorean Life*, *Protrepticus*, *On General Mathematical Science*, fragments of *On the Soul*.

**Metaphysical Hierarchy**
* The One, Intelligible and Intellective Orders, Divine Principles, Ontological Mediation.
* Why must the metaphysical structure of reality contain additional levels beyond Plotinus' three primary hypostases?

**The Soul and Its Embodiment**
* Soul, Embodiment, Human and Divine Intellect, Descent and Ascent.
* Is the human soul permanently connected to the intelligible realm, or does embodiment fundamentally alter its condition?

**Theurgy and Philosophical Knowledge**
* Theurgy (*Theourgia*), Ritual, Divine Symbols, Purification, Spiritual Ascent.
* Can rational contemplation alone unite the soul with divine reality?
* *On the Mysteries*.

**Mathematics and Pythagorean Philosophy**
* Number, Mathematical Structure, Harmony, Philosophical Education.
* Why does mathematical reasoning possess a privileged place in philosophical and spiritual education?

**Philosophical Legacy**
* Plotinus, Porphyry, Proclus, Damascius, Later Neoplatonism.

### Marius Victorinus (c. 300–after 363 CE)

**Position:** Latin Neoplatonism · Early Christian Philosophy · Philosophical Translation

* **Name:** Gaius Marius Victorinus.
* **Life:** Roman rhetorician and philosopher, probably born in Roman North Africa; taught in Rome and converted to Christianity late in life.
* **Contributions:** Helped transmit Greek logical and Neoplatonic concepts into Latin philosophical theology. His work contributed to the intellectual environment of Augustine.
* **Major Works:** *Against Arius (Adversus Arium)*, *To Candidus the Arian*, commentaries on Pauline letters; translations of Greek logical works, largely lost.

**Neoplatonic Metaphysics and Christian Theology**
* Being, Unity, Divine Intellect, Substance, Trinitarian Relations.
* How can Neoplatonic concepts of unity and differentiation illuminate Christian theology?

**Logic, Language, and Philosophical Translation**
* Greek Logical Terminology, Latin Philosophical Vocabulary, Conceptual Translation.
* How does the translation of philosophical terminology reshape later intellectual traditions?

### Gregory of Nyssa ☆ (c. 335–395 CE)

**Position:** Cappadocian Christianity · Christian Platonism · Philosophical Anthropology · Metaphysics

* **Name:** Gregory of Nyssa (Ancient Greek: Γρηγόριος Νύσσης, *Grēgórios Nýssēs*).

**Divine Infinity and the Limits of Knowledge**

* *The Life of Moses*, *Against Eunomius*.

**The Soul and Human Nature**

* *On the Soul and the Resurrection*, *On the Making of Man*.

**Freedom, Evil, and Moral Responsibility**

**Epektasis and Infinite Spiritual Progress**

### Augustine of Hippo ★ (354–430 CE)

**Position:** Patristic Philosophy · Latin Christian Philosophy · Augustinianism · Neoplatonic Influences · Philosophy of Religion

* **Name:** Augustine of Hippo (Latin: Aurelius Augustinus Hipponensis; /ˈɔːɡəstiːn/).
* **Life:** Born in Thagaste, Roman Numidia (present-day Algeria); studied rhetoric, taught in Carthage, Rome, and Milan, converted to Christianity, and became bishop of Hippo Regius. Died during the Vandal siege of Hippo.
* **Contributions:** One of the most influential philosophers of Late Antiquity and a foundational figure in medieval Western thought. Developed an extensive philosophical theology encompassing knowledge, self-consciousness, free will, evil, grace, time, language, history, political community, and divine reality.
* **Major Works:** *Confessions*, *The City of God*, *On the Trinity*, *On Free Choice of the Will*, *On the Teacher*, *On True Religion*, *On Christian Doctrine*, *On the Literal Meaning of Genesis*, *Against the Academicians*, *On Nature and Grace*.

#### Philosophy of Augustine of Hippo

**Philosophical Method and the Search for Truth**
* **Central thesis:** Truth is found by turning inward and then upward. The mind withdraws from dispersion among external things, examines itself, and discovers within itself truths that are not its own but above it. Skepticism can be defeated from within, because certain items of knowledge survive any skeptical doubt. Augustine does not think of philosophy as a neutral discipline separate from faith. Inquiry begins from the authority of belief and aims at the understanding of what is believed.
* **The program of inner ascent:** *On True Religion* 39.72 states the method: "Do not go outside; return into yourself. Truth dwells in the inner human being. And if you find your nature mutable, transcend yourself as well." The movement runs from exterior (bodies, senses) to interior (the mind) to superior (immutable truth, God). It structures the proof of God in *On Free Choice* II, the ascents of *Confessions* VII.10.16 and IX.10.23–25, and the search for the divine image in *On the Trinity* VIII–XV. The *Soliloquies* (I.2.7) narrows the agenda: "I desire to know God and the soul. Nothing more? Nothing at all."
* **Against the Academics:** *Against the Academicians* (386) responds to Academic skepticism, which Augustine knew mainly through Cicero's *Academica*. Arcesilaus and Carneades held that nothing can be known and that the wise person should withhold assent, acting on what is "persuasive" or "truth-like" (*probabile*, *veri simile*). Augustine argues on several fronts.
  * *Wisdom requires truth:* The Academic sage claims to be wise while denying that he knows anything. But wisdom is knowledge of the truth, and the happy life requires possession of what is sought, not endless seeking.
  * *Truth-likeness presupposes truth:* To judge that something is "like the truth," one must know what the truth is, just as one cannot judge that a son resembles his father without knowing the father.
  * *Immune items of knowledge:* Some knowledge survives every skeptical argument. Disjunctions like "there is one world or not one" are certain even if their disjuncts are not (III.10.23). Mathematical truths such as "three times three is nine" hold whether or not one is dreaming (III.11.25). Reports of appearances such as "this seems white to me" cannot be refuted (III.11.26).
  * *The Academics' secret doctrine:* Augustine speculatively suggests (III.17.37–20.43) that the Academics publicly feigned skepticism to protect Platonic doctrine from Stoic materialism. This is a historical conjecture with little support.
* **"Si fallor, sum":** In several works Augustine offers an argument against the skeptic from the self-certainty of existence. "If I am mistaken, I exist; for whoever does not exist cannot be mistaken" (*City of God* XI.26; also *On Free Choice* II.3.7; *On the Trinity* X.10.14, XV.12.21). The argument shows that the skeptic's own doubt or error presupposes the existence and life of the doubter. *On the Trinity* X.10.14 extends it: whoever doubts lives, remembers, understands, wills, thinks, knows, and judges. A doubter may doubt anything else, but cannot doubt these.
  * *Use of the argument:* Augustine does not use it, as Descartes did, as the foundation from which to reconstruct all knowledge. He uses it to refute global skepticism and to reveal the mind's immediate presence to itself.
  * *Testimony:* Augustine explicitly defends knowledge from testimony and from the senses against excessive doubt (XV.12.21). We know that cities exist which we have never seen, and that particular people are our parents. His epistemology is therefore not foundationalist in the Cartesian sense.
* **Faith and reason:** Authority is prior in time, reason prior in reality (*On Order* II.9.26; *On True Religion* 24.45). Most people must begin with belief on authority, but belief seeks understanding. Augustine cites the Old Latin text of Isaiah 7:9, "Unless you believe, you will not understand," and the formula "believe in order to understand" (*crede ut intelligas*, *Tractates on John* 29.6). Yet in a qualified sense reason precedes faith: one must judge rationally which authority to believe (*Letter* 120.3), and faith is itself a kind of thinking with assent (*cum assensione cogitare*). Understanding is the reward of faith.
* **Dialectic and the liberal arts:** The early dialogues propose an ascent through the liberal arts—grammar, dialectic, rhetoric, music, geometry, astronomy, philosophy—as a path from sensible things to intelligible truth (*On Order* II.12.35–19.51). Augustine later regretted his overconfidence in this program (*Retractations* I.3). He came to emphasize instead the soul's moral need for purification and grace.
* **Major objections:** The *si fallor, sum* argument establishes only the existence of the doubter, not the reliability of the senses or the existence of the external world. It answers global skepticism but not more limited doubts. The inward method may also seem to privilege introspection and to presuppose that the mind's discovery of immutable truths within itself implies their transcendence.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine's anti-skeptical arguments, especially *si fallor, sum*, anticipate Descartes's *cogito*. Arnauld noticed the parallel in his Objections to the *Meditations*, and Descartes acknowledged it in correspondence while distinguishing his use of the argument. More fundamentally, Augustine established interiority as a philosophical method: the first-person turn as the road to truth. This shaped medieval contemplative philosophy, Descartes, and phenomenology. His model of faith seeking understanding defined the relation between philosophy and theology in the Latin Middle Ages, notably in Anselm.

**Epistemology and Divine Illumination**: `illuminationism`
* **Central thesis:** Human minds are mutable and finite. Yet they apprehend truths that are necessary, immutable, and common to all minds, the truths of mathematics and of wisdom. Such apprehension cannot be explained by sensory experience or by the mind's own resources. It requires that the mind be illuminated by the eternal Truth, which is God. "The intellectual mind sees intelligible things in a certain incorporeal light of its own kind, as the bodily eye sees what is around it in corporeal light" (*On the Trinity* XII.15.24).
* **The argument of On Free Choice II:** In dialogue with Evodius, Augustine argues for God's existence from the mind's grasp of immutable truths.
  1. There is a hierarchy of existence, life, and understanding. What understands also lives and exists, so it is higher (II.3.7).
  2. The outer senses perceive objects. The "inner sense" (*sensus interior*) perceives the outer senses' activity and judges them. Reason judges both. What judges is superior to what is judged (II.3.8–6.13).
  3. Reason apprehends truths of number ("seven plus three is ten") and rules of wisdom ("we should live justly," "the better should be preferred to the worse"). These are immutable, necessary, and the same for every mind that grasps them (II.8.20–12.34).
  4. These truths are not private to any mind. They are not inferior to the mind, since the mind judges *by* them, not *about* them. They are not equal to it, since they are immutable and it is mutable. Therefore they are superior to the human mind.
  5. Something superior to reason exists. If there is nothing above it, this truth itself is God. If there is something above it, that is God (II.15.39).
* **Assessment of the argument:** The argument's force depends on step 4. It moves from the intersubjective necessity of mathematical and moral truths to the existence of a single superior reality. A critic may reply that such truths are necessary without being substantial entities, or that their objectivity can be explained without a superior mind. Augustine's realism about necessary truths makes the inference compelling within a Platonist framework.
* **On the Teacher:** In *On the Teacher* (389), a dialogue with his son Adeodatus, Augustine argues that no one learns anything from signs alone. A sign teaches only if we already know what it signifies (see the section on language). In learning, we consult the inner Truth that presides over the mind, which is Christ, "the immutable power and eternal wisdom of God" (11.38). Sensible things are learned by perception and intelligible things by consulting the inner light (12.39–40). Teachers only prompt us to consult.
* **Against recollection:** Augustine rejects the Platonic account of learning as recollection of knowledge acquired in a previous life (*On the Trinity* XII.15.24). If the slave in the *Meno* answered correctly because of a previous life, everyone would have been a geometer in a previous life, since everyone can be led to such answers. The better explanation is that the intelligible light of eternal reason is present to every mind.
* **Interpretations of illumination:** Augustine never gives a precise account of how illumination works. Four main interpretations have emerged.
  * *Ontologism:* The mind sees ideas in God's own mind, or sees God directly. Nicolas Malebranche and nineteenth-century ontologists claimed Augustinian ancestry. Augustine denies, however, that we see God's essence in this life.
  * *Concept-supplying:* Illumination supplies the content of concepts, an innate or divinely given conceptual repertoire.
  * *Normative or formal (Gilson, Portalié):* Illumination does not supply concepts. It supplies the certainty, necessity, and normative force of our judgments, the standard by which we judge that something is beautiful, equal, or just.
  * *Naturalized (Aquinas):* God created the intellect with a natural light that participates in the divine light. We know "in the eternal reasons" as in the principle of our knowledge, without any special divine action (*Summa Theologiae* I.84.5).
  
  Ronald Nash and Lydia Schumacher have argued that the naturalized reading distorts Augustine. Others defend it as a legitimate development.
* **Perception and the three kinds of vision:** Sensation is not the body acting on the soul. The soul is superior to the body and cannot be acted on by it. Sensation is the soul's active noticing of changes in its body (*On the Magnitude of the Soul* 23.41; *On Genesis According to the Letter* XII.16.33). Augustine distinguishes three kinds of vision (*On Genesis According to the Letter* XII.6.15–24.51).
  * *Corporeal vision:* Seeing bodies through the eyes.
  * *Spiritual vision:* Seeing images in the imagination, in memory, dreams, and visions.
  * *Intellectual vision:* Seeing intelligible realities such as love, justice, and the mind itself.
  
  Intellectual vision judges the others and is never deceived, although it can fail to see clearly.
* **Knowledge and wisdom:** *On the Trinity* XII–XIII distinguishes knowledge (*scientia*), the rational cognition of temporal things needed for action, from wisdom (*sapientia*), the contemplative cognition of eternal things. Both belong to the rational mind. Knowledge should serve wisdom. Faith in the temporal events of Christ's life belongs to knowledge, but leads to the wisdom of contemplation.
* **Major objections:**
  * The explanatory role of illumination is unclear. If it merely names the fact that we know necessary truths, it explains nothing.
  * If it is a special divine act in each cognition, it threatens the natural integrity of human understanding. Henry of Ghent and Duns Scotus debated this question in the late thirteenth century; Scotus argued that illumination either is natural or produces no certainty.
  * The argument from immutable truths to God depends on a contested realism about abstract objects.
* **Philosophical significance:** Illumination theory was the dominant medieval account of intellectual knowledge until the reception of Aristotle's *De Anima*. Its central problem—how finite minds grasp necessary truths—survives in modern debates about a priori knowledge and mathematical epistemology, such as Benacerraf's problem of access to abstract objects. Augustine's answer represents the theistic version of mathematical and moral realism: necessary truths are grounded in a divine mind.

**Metaphysics of God and Creation**: `classical theism`
* **Central thesis:** God is immutable Being itself, supremely simple, eternal, and good. All other things are created from nothing by God's will, depend on God at every moment for their existence, and are good insofar as they exist. God does not exist in time. He created time together with the world, so there was no time "before" creation.
* **God as true being:** God's name in Exodus 3:14, "I am who am," expresses that God truly is. To be truly is to be immutable: whatever changes "is and is not," since it ceases to be what it was. Creatures "neither wholly are nor wholly are not; they are, because they are from you; they are not, because they are not what you are" (*Confessions* VII.11.17). Augustine calls God "the selfsame" (*idipsum*, IX.10.24). This identification of being with immutability is Platonic in origin. Augustine applies it to the God of Scripture.
* **Divine simplicity:** God is whatever he has. God does not *have* wisdom, goodness, or power as distinct qualities; God *is* his wisdom, goodness, and power (*City of God* XI.10; *On the Trinity* V–VII). The one exception concerns the relations among the divine persons (see the Trinity section). Simplicity follows from immutability: anything composed of distinct properties could in principle lose some of them.
* **Goodness of being:** "Whatever is, is good" (*Confessions* VII.12.18). Every creature has measure, form, and order (*modus*, *species*, *ordo*), the marks of goodness (*On the Nature of the Good* 3). This triad derives from Wisdom 11:21, "you have ordered all things in measure, number, and weight." The more measure, form, and order a thing has, the better it is. Augustine's metaphysics is therefore a hierarchy of degrees of being and goodness.
* **Creation from nothing:** Augustine's doctrine of creation is shaped by two contrasts.
  * *Against the Manichaeans:* There is no second uncreated principle of evil or matter. God made everything.
  * *Against emanation from God's substance:* Creation is not "from God" (*de Deo*), as the Son is generated from the Father. If it were, creatures would be equal to God. Creation is "from nothing" (*de nihilo*) and therefore mutable (*Confessions* XII.7.7; *On the Nature of the Good* 1).
* **Formless matter and simultaneous creation:** God first creates a formless matter, "almost nothing" (*prope nihil*, *Confessions* XII.6.6), which is prior in nature but not in time to formed things. In *On Genesis According to the Letter*, Augustine argues on the basis of Sirach 18:1 ("he created all things at once") that God created all things simultaneously. Things that appear later were created in their "causal" or "seminal reasons" (*rationes causales*, *rationes seminales*). These are potencies placed in creation that unfold over time under providence (VI.10.17; IX.17.32). The six days are not temporal stages but an order of angelic knowledge.
* **Divine ideas:** The Platonic Forms are relocated in the divine mind. Ideas are "principal forms or stable and immutable reasons of things," contained in the divine intelligence, according to which everything that comes to be is formed (*Eighty-Three Different Questions* q. 46). This followed Middle Platonist precedent and became the standard Christian reinterpretation of Plato.
* **Eternity and time:** God is not in time. His "years" do not come and go; his "today" is eternity (*Confessions* XI.13.16). The world was created "with time, not in time" (*City of God* XI.6). Time is the measure of change, so it began with changing creatures.
* **"What was God doing before creation?"** Augustine declines the joke that God was "preparing hell for those who pry into mysteries" (*Confessions* XI.12.14). He answers that the question is ill-formed. There was no "before" before time (XI.13.15).
* **"Why did God create when he did?"** Augustine raises this objection against the eternal will (*City of God* XI.4–5; XII.15–18). His reply has two parts. First, a timeless will can will a temporal effect without a new act of willing in God. Second, the question "why not earlier?" presupposes empty infinite time before the world. That is as confused as asking why God created the world in this place rather than another in an imagined infinite space.
* **Ontological dependence:** Creatures depend on God not only for their beginning but for their continued existence. If God withdrew his creative power, they would cease to be at once (*On Genesis According to the Letter* IV.12.22). Created being is radically contingent.
* **Major objections:**
  * Does divine simplicity make God's will identical with his essence? If so, does that make creation necessary, or make God's knowledge of contingent facts part of his necessary nature? This "modal collapse" problem was debated by later scholastics and contemporary philosophers of religion.
  * The coherence of a timeless will with a temporal effect is disputed.
  * Augustine's equation of being and goodness seems to imply that more being is always better, which is questionable.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine's synthesis of Platonic metaphysics (immutability, degrees of being, Forms) with the Biblical doctrine of creation from nothing became the standard framework of Christian metaphysics. His analysis of the beginning of time and the timelessness of God anticipates modern relational theories of time and discussions of whether "before the Big Bang" is meaningful. The doctrine of divine ideas, the convertibility of being and goodness, and divine simplicity were basic to medieval philosophy and remain central in contemporary philosophy of religion.

**The Problem of Evil**: `privation theory of evil`
* **Central thesis:** Evil is not a substance or positive entity. It is the privation (*privatio*) or corruption of a good that ought to be present. All substances, as created by a good God, are good. Moral evil is the free turning away of a rational will from the immutable good toward mutable goods. God is not the author of sin. The evils suffered as penalties are just, and the order of the whole universe, including its imperfections, is good.
* **The Manichaean background:** For about nine years Augustine was an "auditor" among the Manichaeans. They held a cosmic dualism of two eternal principles, Light (good) and Darkness (evil), and explained human sin as the activity of an evil substance within us. Manichaeism attracted Augustine because it seemed to protect God from responsibility for evil. It also depended on a materialist conception of both God and evil, since Augustine at the time could not conceive of incorporeal substance (*Confessions* V.10.19–20; VII.1.1–2).
* **The Platonist solution (Confessions VII):** Through reading "the books of the Platonists," almost certainly works of Plotinus and Porphyry in Marius Victorinus' Latin translation, Augustine came to conceive of incorporeal reality. He also came to see evil as privation. His argument runs:
  1. Everything that exists is good insofar as it exists, being made by a good God.
  2. Things that are corrupted are deprived of some good. If they had no good, they could not be corrupted, for nothing would be there to be harmed.
  3. If corruption deprived a thing of all good, it would cease to exist altogether.
  4. Therefore evil is not a thing but a deprivation of good (VII.12.18).
  5. When Augustine asked what wickedness is, he found that it is not a substance but "the perversity of a will turned away from the supreme substance, God, toward lower things" (VII.16.22).
  
  The *Enchiridion* (3.11) gives the analogy: in bodies, disease and wounds are nothing but the absence of health. When cured, these evils do not go elsewhere; they cease to exist.
* **Two kinds of evil:** "Evil" means either doing evil or suffering evil (*On Free Choice* I.1.1). God is the author of the second, because just punishment is good. He is not the author of the first. Later tradition calls these evil of fault (*malum culpae*) and evil of penalty (*malum poenae*).
* **Moral evil and free will:** Sin is the will's turning away (*aversio*) from the immutable good, which is common to all, toward mutable and private goods (*On Free Choice* II.19.53). What it turns to is good. The evil is in the disordered turning.
  * *Deficient causation:* The movement of turning away has no efficient cause, because it is a defect. It comes "from nothing" (II.20.54). Seeking the cause of an evil will is like trying to see darkness or hear silence. One perceives them only by the absence of perception (*City of God* XII.7).
  * *The first evil will:* Why did some angels fall and others not, if both were created equally good? Augustine admits that no explanation is available beyond the will itself (XII.6–9). The evil will's origin is opaque by its nature.
* **Why God gave free will:** Free will is a good, though an "intermediate" one (*medium bonum*). It can be used well or badly, but without it no one could live rightly or deserve praise. God gave it for the sake of right living, not for sinning (*On Free Choice* II.1.3; II.18.48–19.50).
* **Natural evil and the order of the whole:** Augustine offers several explanations of natural evils.
  * *Penalty:* Some natural evils are just consequences of sin, such as death and suffering after the Fall.
  * *Natural cycles:* The perishing of mutable things is part of an ordered succession in which each yields to the next, like syllables of a poem (*City of God* XII.4).
  * *Aesthetic order:* The universe as a whole is more beautiful by containing a hierarchy of goods and by contrasts, as a painting needs dark colors or a poem antitheses (XI.18; XI.22–23). This argument was inherited from Plotinus and the Stoics.
* **Major objections:**
  * *The reality of pain:* Pain and suffering seem positive realities, not mere absences. Augustine's reply is that pain is evidence of a good nature resisting corruption. This reply does not remove the experiential reality of suffering.
  * *Explanatory adequacy:* A privation theory explains what evil is, metaphysically. It does not explain why God permits it. That requires the free-will defense and the aesthetic argument.
  * *The first sin:* The defective-cause account leaves the first evil choice unexplained. Critics see this as a confession of mystery rather than an explanation.
  * *The aesthetic argument:* It seems to treat suffering creatures as means to the beauty of the whole.
  * *Later Augustine:* The late doctrines of original sin and predestination complicate the free-will defense (see the next section).
* **Philosophical significance:** The privation theory became the standard Christian metaphysics of evil, adopted by Pseudo-Dionysius, Aquinas, and Leibniz. John Hick distinguished an "Augustinian" theodicy (a good creation spoiled by free creaturely rebellion) from an "Irenaean" one (soul-making). Alvin Plantinga's free-will defense is a modern descendant of Augustine's argument from the goodness of free will. Augustine also transformed the ancient question "where does evil come from?" (*unde malum?*). He removed it from cosmology, where it concerned matter or a second principle, and relocated it in the will.

**Free Will, Sin, and Divine Grace**: `theological compatibilism (contested)`
* **Central thesis:** Human beings have free choice of the will (*liberum arbitrium*) and are responsible for their sins. But after the first sin, human nature is so damaged that without God's grace no one can will or do the good that leads to salvation. True freedom (*libertas*) is not the capacity to choose either way but the freedom to do good, which grace restores. Salvation depends entirely on God's gratuitous election, made before and independently of human merit. Augustine holds that this predestination is compatible with human freedom and responsibility.
* **Liberum arbitrium and libertas:**
  * *Liberum arbitrium:* The natural capacity of the will to choose. Sinners retain it, but it is free only for sinning unless grace frees it.
  * *Libertas:* The freedom to choose and delight in the good.
  
  The distinction is summarized in the "four states" of human freedom (*On Rebuke and Grace* 12.33; later systematized by Peter Lombard):
  1. Adam before the Fall: able not to sin (*posse non peccare*).
  2. Fallen humanity: not able not to sin (*non posse non peccare*).
  3. Humanity under grace: again able not to sin.
  4. The blessed in heaven: not able to sin (*non posse peccare*).
  
  The highest freedom is the inability to sin. This shows that Augustine does not identify freedom with indeterminate choice.
* **The early defense of free will (On Free Choice):**
  * *The will is in our power:* "Nothing is so much in our power as the will itself" (III.3.7). Sin is voluntary and therefore justly punished.
  * *Foreknowledge (III.2.4–4.11):* Evodius asks whether God's foreknowledge of sin makes it necessary. Augustine replies that God foreknows that we will will. Since what he foreknows is our willing, our willing remains a willing. "Our will would not be a will if it were not in our power. Since it is in our power, it is free" (III.3.8). Just as memory of the past does not cause past events, foreknowledge does not cause future ones (III.4.11).
  * *Against Cicero (City of God V.9–10):* Cicero denied foreknowledge to save free will. Augustine affirms both. Our wills are among the causes in the order of causes that God foreknows.
* **Original sin:** All humans sinned "in Adam." Augustine's Latin text of Romans 5:12 read "in whom (*in quo*) all sinned," where the Greek *eph' hō* more likely means "because." Human nature is corrupted and guilty. Sin is transmitted by generation, through concupiscence, disordered desire. Humanity is a "mass of perdition" (*massa damnata*, *massa peccati*, *To Simplician* I.2.16). Infants who die unbaptized are condemned, though to the mildest punishment.
* **The turning point of 396:** In *To Simplician* I.2, Augustine interprets Romans 9 ("Jacob I have loved, Esau I have hated") and concludes that even the beginning of faith (*initium fidei*) is God's gift. God's call is "congruous": it is given in a way that God knows will be accepted. In the *Retractations* (II.1) he reflects: "I labored on behalf of the free choice of the human will, but the grace of God won out." The *Confessions* express the new view in the prayer "Give what you command, and command what you will" (*da quod iubes et iube quod vis*, X.29.40). Augustine reports that this prayer provoked Pelagius.
* **The Pelagian controversy (from 411):** Pelagius, Caelestius, and later Julian of Eclanum held three main theses.
  * Human nature, created good, retains the capacity to avoid sin.
  * Adam's sin harmed humanity by bad example, not by inherited guilt.
  * Grace consists of nature, the law, the forgiveness of sins, and the example of Christ.
  
  Augustine replied that grace is an interior help that heals the will and inspires delight (*delectatio*) in the good. It "operates" that we may will, and "cooperates" when we will (*On Grace and Free Choice* 17.33). Without it, knowledge of the law increases sin. Julian accused Augustine of Manichaeism, because he made human nature evil and sex sinful. Augustine replied that nature is good but wounded.
* **Predestination:** God chooses, from the mass of humanity, a fixed number for salvation by unmerited mercy. He gives them the gift of perseverance. He leaves the rest in the condemnation they justly deserve. This is a "passing over" rather than active causation of their sin. The statement in 1 Timothy 2:4 that "God wills all people to be saved" is reinterpreted to mean "all kinds of people" (*Enchiridion* 27.103). Monks at Hadrumetum and Marseilles, the so-called "semi-Pelagians" including John Cassian, objected that this made exhortation and rebuke pointless. Augustine answered in *On Rebuke and Grace*, *On the Predestination of the Saints*, and *On the Gift of Perseverance*.
* **Responsibility and the bound will:** How can people who are "not able not to sin" be responsible?
  * *Augustine's answer:* The necessity of sinning is not coercion. Sinners sin willingly, from their own will, even if they cannot will otherwise without grace. Responsibility attaches to acting from one's will, not to having alternative possibilities.
  * *Responsibility for original sin:* Augustine grounds it in humanity's real participation in Adam, an idea of collective agency that most modern readers find hard to accept.
* **Interpretive debate:** Was Augustine a libertarian who became a compatibilist?
  * *Change of view:* John Rist argued that the late Augustine effectively abandoned meaningful human freedom. Jesse Couenhoven argued that the late Augustine rejected the free-will defense.
  * *Continuity:* Augustine always understood freedom primarily as freedom for the good, and never held a libertarian conception of choice in the modern sense. The early *On Free Choice* addressed the origin of evil against the Manichaeans, not the question of grace.
  * *Augustine's own view:* He insisted on his consistency (*Retractations* I.9), though the Pelagians quoted his early work against him.
* **Major objections:**
  * Predestination of a fixed number, with the remainder left to damnation, seems incompatible with divine goodness and justice.
  * The notion of inherited guilt is morally problematic.
  * If grace is efficacious and irresistible, praise and blame seem pointless.
  * Responsibility without alternative possibilities is defensible in modern compatibilist terms. It is much harder to defend when the agent's will has been determined by a prior collective sin.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings set the terms for later Christian debates about grace and freedom: the Carolingian controversy over Gottschalk, the Reformation (Luther's *The Bondage of the Will* against Erasmus, Calvin), Jansenism (Jansen's *Augustinus*, Pascal), and the Catholic debates between Molinists and Thomists. His distinction between freedom as choice and freedom as the good condition of the will remains philosophically important. It anticipates hierarchical and "reasons-responsive" theories of freedom, and the idea, found in Spinoza, Kant, and Hegel, that true freedom is a kind of rational self-determination rather than indifference.

**Memory, Self-Consciousness, and the Inner Life**: `substance dualism`
* **Central thesis:** The mind (*mens*) is an incorporeal substance, immediately present to itself and certain of its own existence and activities. Memory (*memoria*) is not merely a store of past impressions but the mind's whole presence to itself, including its latent knowledge and its relation to God. Self-knowledge has a distinctive structure. The mind always knows itself, though it does not always think about itself.
* **Memory in Confessions X:** Augustine describes memory as "fields and spacious palaces" (*campos et lata praetoria*, X.8.12) containing countless images. Its contents are of several kinds.
  * *Images of sensible things:* These are stored and can be recalled at will.
  * *Intelligible contents:* The liberal arts, numbers, and the laws of dimensions are present in memory not as images but as the things themselves (X.9.16–12.19). They were already there, "hidden in remote caves," before anyone taught them.
  * *Emotions:* Remembering joy without feeling joy is possible. Memory is like the "stomach of the mind" (X.14.21), which holds food but does not taste it.
  * *Forgetfulness:* Remembering forgetfulness is paradoxical. When I remember forgetfulness, forgetfulness is present to memory. But forgetfulness is the absence of memory. How can it be present (X.16.24–25)? Augustine leaves the puzzle partly unresolved.
* **Seeking the happy life and God in memory:** All people desire the happy life (*beata vita*). They must therefore somehow know it, and it must be somehow in memory (X.20.29–23.34). Augustine concludes that it is the joy of truth, *gaudium de veritate*. God is in memory not as an image, nor as an affection, nor as the mind itself, but as the one whom the mind has learned. "Since I learned you, you remain in my memory" (X.24.35–26.37). The search through memory thus culminates in the recognition that God is above memory and yet present to it: "Late have I loved you, beauty so ancient and so new" (X.27.38).
* **The mind as a question to itself:** "I have become a question to myself" (*mihi quaestio factus sum*, X.33.50; compare IV.4.9). Self-examination reveals a depth of the self that exceeds self-knowledge. "I cannot grasp all that I am" (X.8.15). Augustine combines certainty about the self's existence with acknowledgment of its opacity.
* **Self-knowledge in On the Trinity X:**
  * *The puzzle:* How can the mind seek to know itself? If it does not know itself, it cannot know what to seek. If it already knows itself, it need not seek. Augustine answers that the mind cannot know itself partly. When it knows itself, it knows itself whole (X.4.6).
  * *Self-knowledge versus self-thought:* Augustine distinguishes "to know oneself" (*se nosse*) from "to think of oneself" (*se cogitare*) (X.5.7; XIV.6.8). The mind always knows itself, but it does not always focus on itself in thought. It can confuse itself with what it loves, such as bodies and images. This distinction anticipates the modern contrast between pre-reflective self-awareness and reflective self-consciousness.
  * *The argument for immateriality (X.10.13–16):*
    1. The mind is certain that it lives, remembers, understands, wills, thinks, knows, and judges.
    2. The mind is uncertain whether it is air, fire, brain, or some other body.
    3. What is known with certainty to belong to the mind's substance cannot be identical to what it is uncertain of.
    4. Therefore the mind is not air, fire, or brain. It is the substance it knows with certainty, an immaterial one.
    
    The argument is structurally parallel to Descartes's argument from the *Meditations*. It faces the same objection: the "masked man fallacy," an illegitimate inference from what is known *under one description* to what *is* the case. Augustine adds a further premise: the mind knows its own substance when it knows itself. If that premise is granted, the argument is stronger than its Cartesian counterpart, but it is precisely the disputed premise.
* **Soul and body:** Augustine defines the human being as "a rational soul using a mortal and earthly body" (*On the Morals of the Catholic Church* I.27.52), a strongly Platonic formulation. In later works, especially *City of God* XIII–XIV and XXII, he insists that the human being is soul and body together. The body is not a prison but a good creation, and the resurrection restores the whole person.
* **Origin of the soul:** Augustine never settled whether each soul is created individually (creationism) or transmitted from the parents (traducianism). The question bore on the transmission of original sin. He discussed it with Jerome (*Letter* 166) and left it open in the *Retractations*.
* **Personal identity:** Augustine's analysis of memory provides a framework for the continuity of the self. His reconstruction of his own infancy in *Confessions* I depends on testimony, because he cannot remember it (I.6.7–7.12). He does not, however, propose a memory criterion of personal identity in the Lockean sense.
* **Major objections:**
  * The argument for immateriality depends on contested premises about self-knowledge.
  * Augustine's claim that the mind knows itself "whole" is difficult to reconcile with his own emphasis on the self's opacity.
  * The status of intelligible contents "in" memory is unclear: do they belong to the mind, or are they truths it is related to?
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine was the first philosopher to make the first-person standpoint and the inner life central objects of philosophical analysis. His treatment of memory influenced Bergson, Husserl, and Proust. His distinction between implicit self-knowledge and explicit self-thought anticipates phenomenological accounts of self-consciousness. His arguments for the certainty and immateriality of the mind anticipate Descartes, whose relation to Augustine was noticed by contemporaries.

**The Philosophy of Time**: `presentism (qualified)`
* **Central thesis:** Time is real but paradoxical. The past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist. The present has no duration. Yet we measure time. Augustine's resolution is that time is measured in the mind, as a "distension" (*distentio*) of the soul. The past exists as memory, the present as attention, and the future as expectation.
* **The aporia (Confessions XI.14.17–20.26):** "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know. If I wish to explain it to one who asks, I do not know" (XI.14.17). The difficulties can be laid out step by step.
  1. The past is no longer and the future is not yet. So neither exists.
  2. The present, if it were always present and did not pass into the past, would be eternity, not time. It is time only because it tends toward not-being.
  3. The present cannot have extension. Any extended present—a century, a year, a day, an hour—divides into parts some of which are past and some future. The present shrinks to a durationless instant (XI.15.18–20).
  4. Yet we speak of long and short times and measure them. What is measured cannot be what does not exist or what has no extension.
* **The three presents (XI.20.26):** It is not strictly correct to say there are three times. One should say there are three presents: a present of past things, which is memory; a present of present things, which is attention (*contuitus*); and a present of future things, which is expectation. These exist in the mind.
* **Time is not motion:** Augustine rejects the identification of time with the motion of the heavenly bodies (XI.23.29–24.31). If the heavens stopped while a potter's wheel turned, we could still measure its revolutions. When the sun stood still for Joshua, the battle continued through time. We measure motion by time, not time by motion.
* **Distension of the mind (XI.26.33–28.38):** Time "is nothing other than a distension, but of what I do not know. It would be surprising if it were not of the mind itself" (XI.26.33). When we measure a sound, we do not measure the sound, which has passed. We measure the impression (*affectio*) it leaves, which remains in the mind (XI.27.36). When I recite a psalm, my expectation stretches over the whole. As I proceed, what passes from expectation through attention into memory increases memory and decreases expectation (XI.28.38). The same structure applies to a whole life and to all of human history.
* **Interpretations:**
  * *Psychological subjectivism:* Time is mind-dependent. It exists only as the soul's distension.
  * *Epistemological reading:* Augustine explains how time is measured, not what time is in itself. Elsewhere he says that time began with the movements of creatures (*City of God* XI.6; *On Genesis According to the Letter* V.5.12). That is an objective, cosmological account, which seems to imply that time existed before human minds.
  * *Teske's reading:* Roland Teske argued that the *distentio animi* may refer to a world soul, following Plotinus' account of time as "the life of soul" (*Enneads* III.7.11). This would make time objective and psychological at once. Augustine was undecided about the world soul.
* **Ethical dimension:** The concluding chapters (XI.29.39) contrast the soul's dispersion in time (*distentio*), being "scattered in times," with a spiritual "extension" (*extentio*) and "intention" (*intentio*) toward the eternal God. Time is not only a metaphysical problem but a condition of fragmentation that the soul seeks to overcome.
* **Major objections:**
  * The account presupposes what it explains. Expectation passing into memory is itself a temporal process, and the distension itself has duration.
  * Augustine's reduction of the present to an instant is itself questionable. Experienced time has duration, as later theories of the "specious present" (William James) emphasized.
  * If time is in the mind, the earlier cosmological claims about time beginning with creation conflict with the psychological account.
* **Philosophical significance:** *Confessions* XI is among the most influential texts in the philosophy of time.
  * *Husserl:* His *Lectures on the Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness* begins by praising it. His analysis of retention, primal impression, and protention reformulates Augustine's three presents.
  * *Wittgenstein:* *Philosophical Investigations* §89 cites the "If no one asks me" passage as a paradigm of a philosophical puzzle arising from the grammar of our concepts.
  * *Ricoeur:* *Time and Narrative* uses Augustine's *distentio* and *intentio* to analyze narrative as the configuration of temporal experience.
  
  The tension between psychological and cosmological time remains central to the metaphysics of time.

**The Will, Desire, and Moral Psychology**: `voluntarism`
* **Central thesis:** The will (*voluntas*) is the central faculty of human agency. Love (*amor*) is the will's fundamental orientation: we become what we love. Moral failure is primarily a defect of the will, not of knowledge. A person can see the good clearly and yet fail to will it wholly, because habit and disordered loves divide the will against itself.
* **The divided will (Confessions VIII):** In the garden at Milan, Augustine recognized the truth of the Christian life but could not will it fully. "My two wills, one old and one new, one carnal and one spiritual, fought each other" (VIII.5.10). The chain of habit binds him: "from a perverse will came lust; serving lust became habit; habit not resisted became necessity" (VIII.5.10).
  * *The paradox (VIII.9.21):* "The mind commands the body and is instantly obeyed. The mind commands itself and is resisted." This is "monstrous." Augustine's solution is that the mind "does not will wholly" (*non ex toto vult*). If it willed wholly, it would not need to command itself to will. It is not a monster to partly will and partly not; it is a sickness of the mind (*aegritudo animi*).
  * *Against the Manichaeans (VIII.10.22–24):* Two wills do not prove two natures, one good and one evil. Two wills can conflict in choosing between several goods, such as going to the theater or to church. The division lies within one soul.
* **Comparison with ancient akrasia:** Socrates denied akrasia, and Aristotle explained it as a failure of practical knowledge. For Augustine, the problem is not ignorance but a will divided against itself, sustained by habit. Knowing the good does not suffice. One must delight in it: "we necessarily act according to what delights us more" (*Exposition of Galatians* 49). Grace works by giving delight in the good. Scholars such as Albrecht Dihle and Charles Kahn have credited Augustine with "discovering" the will as a distinct faculty. Michael Frede argued that the notion of a free will originated earlier, in Stoicism (especially Epictetus), and that Augustine transformed rather than invented it.
* **The theft of the pears (Confessions II.4.9–10.18):** As an adolescent Augustine stole pears he did not want, and threw them to pigs. He did it for the pleasure of doing what was forbidden: "I loved my own destruction." He analyzes the theft carefully. It was done in company, which he would not have done alone. It was a perverse imitation of God's omnipotence, the freedom to do what is not permitted (II.6.14). The case shows the will's capacity to choose evil apparently for its own sake. Even so, Augustine finds a distorted pursuit of a good, such as freedom or companionship, in the act.
* **Love as weight:** "My weight is my love. By it I am carried wherever I am carried" (*pondus meum amor meus*, *Confessions* XIII.9.10). Like physical bodies seeking their natural place, the soul moves according to its love. Its rest is in God: "our heart is restless until it rests in you" (I.1.1).
* **The will in cognition and emotion:**
  * *Cognition:* The will directs attention (*intentio*). In perception, the will joins the sense to its object (*On the Trinity* XI.2.2–5).
  * *Emotion:* Emotions are forms of will. Desire, joy, fear, and sadness are "nothing but wills" (*City of God* XIV.6). Against the Stoic ideal of *apatheia*, Augustine argues that emotions are good if the love behind them is good (XIV.9). Christ felt grief and anger.
* **Major objections:**
  * The notion of a divided will risks incoherence. If a single will can will and not will the same thing, what makes it one faculty?
  * Augustine's account of habit as "necessity" threatens responsibility. He answers that the necessity was built by earlier free choices.
  * The claim that emotions are wills seems to overintellectualize, or over-volitionalize, affective life.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine's analysis of the divided will and of habit made the will central to Western moral psychology. It shaped medieval voluntarism (Scotus, Ockham), Luther's account of bondage, and Kant's practical philosophy. Contemporary discussions of hierarchical agency are often compared with Augustine's account, including Harry Frankfurt's distinction between first-order desires and second-order volitions and his account of the "unwilling addict." The account of love as orientation of the whole self influenced Pascal's "order of the heart," Heidegger's analysis of care, and Arendt's study of love and will.

**Love, Virtue, and the Moral Order**: `eudaimonism`
* **Central thesis:** All human beings seek happiness. Happiness consists in possessing and enjoying the highest good, which is God, an eternal and unchangeable good that cannot be lost against one's will. Virtue is rightly ordered love (*ordo amoris*). Things are to be loved according to their place in the order of being, with God loved for his own sake and everything else loved in relation to God. Sin is disordered love, rooted in pride.
* **Happiness:** In *On the Happy Life* (386), the happy person is one who possesses what he wants, wants nothing wrongly, and possesses something that cannot be taken away. Only God satisfies these conditions. *City of God* XIX.1–4 surveys Varro's classification of 288 possible philosophical positions on the highest good. Augustine argues that all philosophical attempts to locate happiness in this life fail. Virtue itself consists in constant warfare against vice. The Stoic sage who permits suicide in extreme misery implicitly admits that life is not happy (XIX.4). Final happiness is eschatological: eternal life in peace.
* **Use and enjoyment:** *On Christian Doctrine* I.3.3–5.5 distinguishes enjoying (*frui*), which is clinging to something with love for its own sake, from using (*uti*), which is referring something to what one loves. Only God is to be enjoyed; everything else is to be used, that is, loved with reference to God (I.22.20).
  * *The problem:* Must the neighbor be "used"? Augustine applies "use" to love of neighbor, and later prefers the phrase "to enjoy in the Lord" (*frui in Domino*, I.33.37). Anders Nygren argued that Augustine's *caritas* synthesizes Platonic *eros*, which is acquisitive desire, with Christian *agapē*, which is gift-love, and thereby distorts the latter. Oliver O'Donovan and others defend Augustine. On their view, loving the neighbor "for God's sake" does not instrumentalize the neighbor. It wills for the neighbor the highest good, God, and loves the neighbor as a fellow participant in it.
* **The order of love:** A "brief and true definition of virtue is: the order of love" (*City of God* XV.22). We should love four things: what is above us (God), ourselves, what is beside us (neighbor), and what is below us (the body) (*On Christian Doctrine* I.23.22). Each is to be loved neither more nor less than it deserves. Disorder consists in loving lesser goods as if they were the highest.
* **Virtues as forms of love:** The four cardinal virtues are redefined as forms of love of God (*On the Morals of the Catholic Church* I.15.25).
  * *Temperance:* love giving itself entirely to the beloved.
  * *Fortitude:* love bearing all things for the sake of the beloved.
  * *Justice:* love serving only the beloved and therefore ruling well.
  * *Prudence:* love wisely distinguishing what helps from what hinders it.
* **Pride and self-love:** Pride (*superbia*) is the beginning of all sin (Sirach 10:13). It is a perverse desire for exaltation, making oneself one's own principle rather than adhering to God (*City of God* XIV.13). Self-love is not bad in itself. One who loves God rightly loves himself, since loving oneself rightly means willing one's true good, which is God. "Love, and do what you will" (*dilige et quod vis fac*, *Tractates on the First Letter of John* 7.8) expresses the priority of rightly ordered love over rules.
* **Pagan virtue:** Virtues not referred to God are "vices rather than virtues" (*City of God* XIX.25). Roman virtues served the love of glory, which restrained worse vices but was itself a vice (V.12–19). The phrase "splendid vices" often attributed to Augustine is not his, but it summarizes this view.
* **Major objections:**
  * The use/enjoyment distinction seems to instrumentalize persons.
  * The eudaimonist structure seems to make even love of God self-interested, which the Kantian tradition criticized.
  * The denial of genuine pagan virtue is harsh. Augustine acknowledges that pagan virtues serve the earthly city and are relatively good.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine transformed ancient eudaimonism into an ethics of love. He gave priority to the orientation of the will over the rational ordering of desire. He made happiness transcendent and eschatological, and he defined the virtues relative to a personal God. The concept of ordered love shaped medieval ethics, Pascal, and Max Scheler's material value ethics (Scheler wrote on *ordo amoris*). The debates about use and enjoyment, eros and agapē, and self-love versus other-love remain central in theological ethics and the philosophy of love.

**Language, Signs, and Interpretation**: `mentalist semantics`
* **Central thesis:** Everything is either a thing (*res*) or a sign (*signum*), which is a thing used to signify something else. Words are the most important signs. Yet words do not teach by themselves. They prompt us to look to the things themselves, either perceived through the senses or understood by consulting the inner Truth. Interpretation, especially of Scripture, must be governed by the rule of charity, love of God and neighbor.
* **Things and signs:** "A sign is a thing which, besides the impression it makes on the senses, causes something else to come into the mind" (*On Christian Doctrine* II.1.1). Signs are either natural, such as smoke signifying fire, or "given" (*data*). Given signs are used by living beings to show "the motions of their minds"—what they feel or think (II.1.2–2.3). Words are conventional given signs. Augustine thus offers a general theory of signs that includes language as a special case. It is a precursor of later semiotics.
* **On the Teacher:** In dialogue with Adeodatus, Augustine raises a series of puzzles.
  * *Signs of signs:* Some words are signs of other signs: "noun" is a sign of nouns.
  * *Function words:* What does "nothing" signify? Or "from"? Perhaps an affection of the mind (2.3–4).
  * *Ostension:* Can anything be shown without signs, by pointing or by doing it? Walking can be shown by walking, but how will the observer know whether you are showing walking, hurrying, or moving?
  * *The conclusion (10.33–12.40):* Nothing is learned through signs. If I already know the thing, the sign teaches me nothing new. If I do not know it, the sign cannot teach me what it signifies. Augustine's example is the word *sarabarae*, "head-coverings" (Daniel 3:94 in the Latin Bible). I learn what the word means only when I see or understand the thing. Words remind us or prompt us to inquire. Teaching occurs when the learner, prompted by words, consults the inner Truth.
* **Wittgenstein:** The *Philosophical Investigations* opens with Augustine's description of how he learned language as a child: adults named objects and pointed to them (*Confessions* I.8.13). Wittgenstein treats this as a "particular picture of the essence of human language," in which words name objects and sentences are combinations of names. He argues that it presupposes the child already has a language of thought, so it explains nothing. Myles Burnyeat argued that *On the Teacher* anticipates Wittgenstein's criticism of ostension. Augustine already argued that pointing is ambiguous and that signs presuppose understanding.
* **The inner word:** In *On the Trinity* XV.10.19–11.20, Augustine distinguishes spoken words from the "inner word" (*verbum mentis*). The inner word is the thought formed from our knowledge, prior to any particular language, neither Greek nor Latin. Spoken words are signs of these inner words. The analogy illustrates the divine Word, generated by the Father without any change. The doctrine of the inner word anticipates later theories of mental language, such as Ockham's, and of concepts as language-independent.
* **Scriptural hermeneutics (On Christian Doctrine):**
  * *Literal versus figurative:* Whatever in Scripture cannot be referred literally to good morals or true faith should be read figuratively (III.10.14).
  * *The rule of charity:* Anyone who thinks he understands Scripture but does not build up the double love of God and neighbor has not understood it. Someone who derives a charitable meaning that the author did not intend is mistaken, but not dangerously so, "like a traveler who leaves the road but reaches the destination through a field" (I.36.40–41).
  * *Multiple true senses:* Augustine accepts that a text may have several true meanings, even if the author intended only one, provided each agrees with truth and charity (*Confessions* XII.18.27–32.43).
  * *Pagan learning:* Christians may appropriate pagan learning, like the Israelites taking the Egyptians' gold (*On Christian Doctrine* II.40.60).
* **Major objections:** The conclusion of *On the Teacher*, that nothing is learned through signs, is paradoxical. Testimony, which Augustine elsewhere defends, seems to be learning through signs. Augustine distinguishes believing on testimony from knowing by understanding, but the boundary is unclear. The rule of charity, though attractive, risks making interpretation unfalsifiable: any interpretation that promotes charity may seem acceptable.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine's sign theory was the foundation of medieval semantics and logic of signification. *On Christian Doctrine* became the basic text of medieval hermeneutics and remained influential for Reformation and modern biblical interpretation. Gadamer and Ricoeur discussed Augustine's role in the history of hermeneutics. His analysis of ostension and of the gap between signs and understanding anticipates central themes of twentieth-century philosophy of language.

**Political Philosophy and the Two Cities**: `political realism`
* **Central thesis:** Humanity is divided into two "cities," defined not by institutions but by what they love. The city of God (*civitas Dei*) is formed by love of God extending to contempt of self. The earthly city (*civitas terrena*) is formed by love of self extending to contempt of God (*City of God* XIV.28). In this age the two cities are intermingled (*permixtae*). No political community, Christian or pagan, embodies true justice or the highest good. Political order is nonetheless a real good: it secures the earthly peace that both cities use.
* **Context:** The sack of Rome by Alaric's Goths in 410 provoked pagan claims that the abandonment of the old gods had brought disaster. Books I–X refute these claims. Books XI–XXII narrate the origin, course, and destined ends of the two cities.
* **The two cities and their institutions:** The cities are not identical with the Church and the state. The Church contains reprobate members, and the earthly city's citizens may become members of the city of God. Who belongs to which city is known only to God. Rome and Babylon are the earthly city's paradigms, but no empirical institution is simply identical with either city.
* **Justice and the commonwealth:** In *On the Republic*, Cicero, through Scipio, defined a commonwealth (*res publica*) as "the people's affair." A people is "a multitude united by consent to law (*iuris consensus*) and community of interest" (*City of God* II.21; XIX.21). Augustine argues that Rome never was a commonwealth in this sense. Law (*ius*) presupposes justice, and there is no justice where God is not given his due.
* **Redefinition (XIX.24):** Augustine offers an alternative definition: "a people is a multitude of rational beings united by a common agreement on the objects of their love." This definition is descriptive rather than normative. It allows Rome and other states to count as peoples. The better the objects of a people's love, the better the people. Political communities are evaluated by what they love, not defined by perfect justice.
* **Kingdoms and robbers (IV.4):** "Without justice, what are kingdoms but great robberies?" Augustine tells of a pirate who answered Alexander the Great: "Because I do it with a small ship I am called a pirate; because you do it with a great fleet you are called emperor."
  * *The tension:* The redefinition of "people" seems to allow that a robber band is a political community.
  * *A reading:* Augustine's point is that political order without true justice differs from a robber band only in scale and stability. That difference is nonetheless real and valuable.
* **Peace:** Peace is "the tranquility of order" (*tranquillitas ordinis*, XIX.13), and every creature seeks it in some form. The earthly city seeks earthly peace for its own sake. The heavenly city "uses" earthly peace during its pilgrimage and obeys the laws that secure it, so long as they do not hinder worship of the true God (XIX.17).
* **Political authority:** God made humans to rule over irrational animals, not over other humans (XIX.15). Political domination and slavery are consequences of sin. Political authority is remedial and coercive: it restrains the effects of sin. This view grounds a realism about the limits of politics. Political order cannot make people good. It can only limit evil.
* **Coercion and war:**
  * *Coercion:* In the Donatist controversy, Augustine came to endorse state coercion of schismatics, citing "compel them to come in" (Luke 14:23) in *Letters* 93 and 185. His justification was that fear can lead people to consider the truth, and many coerced Donatists later rejoiced. This became one of the most controversial elements of his legacy, invoked to justify later persecutions.
  * *Just war:* Augustine developed elements of just war theory. War can be just to avenge injury, under legitimate authority, with the right intention of peace (*Against Faustus* XXII.74–75). Even the wise man waging just war laments its necessity (*City of God* XIX.7).
* **Interpretations of Augustine's politics:**
  * *"Political Augustinianism" (Arquillière):* This medieval tendency, criticized by H.-X. Arquillière, absorbed the state into the Church and subordinated temporal to spiritual power. It distorts Augustine, who did not identify the city of God with the Church.
  * *R. A. Markus, Saeculum:* Augustine's view implies a neutral "secular" sphere, an overlap of the two cities, in which political institutions are neither sacred nor demonic.
  * *Christian realism (Reinhold Niebuhr):* Augustine's realism about human sinfulness is an antidote to political idealism.
  * *Radical Orthodoxy (John Milbank):* Augustine's Church is itself the true polis. Milbank rejects the notion of an autonomous secular sphere.
  * *Liberal Augustinians (Eric Gregory):* Augustine's ethics of love can ground a liberal democratic politics.
* **Major objections:**
  * The redefinition of "people" seems to strip political communities of normative criteria.
  * Augustine accepted slavery as a consequence of sin and a punishment, rather than as an injustice to be abolished.
  * His justification of coercion of heretics contradicts his own account of faith as free assent and of grace as interior.
* **Philosophical significance:** *City of God* is the founding text of Western political theology. It distinguished political order from ultimate salvation and denied that any state embodies the highest good. It also gave political authority a limited but real value. This shaped medieval debates about the two powers (from Pope Gelasius I's letter of 494), the modern idea of the secular, Christian realism, and contemporary debates about religion and liberal democracy.

**History, Providence, and Human Destiny**: `providentialism`
* **Central thesis:** History is not cyclical but linear. It has a beginning in creation, a central event in the Incarnation, and an end in the last judgment and eternal life. It is governed by divine providence, which orders all events, including free human acts, toward their end. But the meaning of history is not legible from empirical events. Only Scripture reveals the course of the two cities, and secular history is ambiguous.
* **Against cyclical time (City of God XII.14–21):** Some philosophers hold that the same events recur in endless cycles. Augustine argues that this makes true happiness impossible: the blessed would know that their happiness will end. It also conflicts with the uniqueness of Christ, who "died once for sins" (Romans 6:9). Augustine adds that God's infinite knowledge can comprehend an unrepeated infinity of new things, so cycles are not needed to make history intelligible.
* **The ages of history:** Augustine divides history into six ages (*aetates*). He correlates them with the six days of creation and the six stages of a human life, from infancy to old age. The first runs from Adam to the Flood; the sixth from Christ to the end. The seventh age is the eternal Sabbath, and the eighth day is the eternal life of the resurrection (*City of God* XXII.30; *On Genesis against the Manichaeans* I.23.35–41).
* **The opacity of secular history:** Unlike Eusebius of Caesarea, who saw the Christian Roman Empire as the providential fulfillment of history, Augustine refused to identify any political success with the progress of the city of God. R. A. Markus showed that this was a substantial shift in Augustine's own thought after about 400. Augustine's friend Orosius wrote a more triumphalist history at Augustine's request, *Seven Books of History against the Pagans*. The two works differ markedly in assessing Rome.
* **Providence and fate:** Augustine rejects astrological fate (*City of God* V.1–8). His argument uses twins such as Esau and Jacob, who were born at nearly the same moment yet lived very different lives. He also rejects the Stoic concept of fate as a necessary chain of causes, unless "fate" simply means the will of God. Providence extends to the smallest things, down to the feathers of birds and the leaves of plants (V.11). Our wills are among the causes in the order that God foreknows. They are therefore not excluded from providence but included in it (V.9–10).
* **Eschatology:**
  * *The millennium:* Augustine rejects a literal thousand-year earthly reign of Christ. The "millennium" of Revelation 20 is the present age of the Church, and the "first resurrection" is the spiritual resurrection of souls (*City of God* XX.7–9). This interpretation became the standard Catholic position.
  * *The final ends:* The final judgment separates the two cities. The damned undergo eternal bodily punishment (XXI). Augustine argues against Origen's hope of universal restoration (*apokatastasis*). The blessed rise in spiritual but real bodies (XXII).
  * *Final rest:* "There we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise. Behold what will be in the end without end" (XXII.30).
* **Major objections:**
  * A providential interpretation of history is unfalsifiable, since any event can be interpreted as providential.
  * Providence over evils raises the problem of evil anew at the level of history.
  * The doctrine of eternal punishment has been criticized as incompatible with divine goodness and justice.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine's linear, providential conception of history shaped Christian historiography and, arguably, modern philosophies of history. Karl Löwith's *Meaning in History* (1949) argued that modern ideas of progress (Hegel, Marx) are secularized forms of Christian eschatology. Hans Blumenberg's *The Legitimacy of the Modern Age* (1966) disputed this "secularization thesis," arguing that modernity is an original self-assertion rather than a transformed theology. Augustine's insistence on the opacity of secular history is also a resource for critics of historical teleology.

**Trinity and Philosophical Theology**: `Nicene trinitarianism`
* **Central thesis:** God is one substance (*substantia*, *essentia*) in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. They are equal and co-eternal and act inseparably. The persons are distinguished only by their relations of origin. The Father begets, the Son is begotten, and the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. The human mind, created in God's image, offers analogies to this unity in trinity, especially the triad of memory, understanding, and will.
* **Structure of On the Trinity:** Augustine wrote the work over about two decades (c. 399–c. 422). Books I–IV establish the doctrine from Scripture. Books V–VII analyze its logic. Books VIII–XV search for the image of the Trinity in the human mind.
* **The logic of relations (Books V–VII):**
  * *The Arian argument:* Whatever is said of God is said substantially, since God has no accidents. "Unbegotten" is said of the Father and "begotten" of the Son. So Father and Son differ in substance.
  * *Augustine's reply:* Some predicates are said neither according to substance nor according to accident, but according to relation (*ad aliquid*, V.5.6). "Father" and "Son" are relative terms. These relations are not accidents, because they are eternal and unchangeable. So relational distinctions do not entail substantial differences.
  * *Substantial predicates:* Predicates said substantially apply to the three in the singular. The Father is great, the Son is great, the Spirit is great—yet not three greats but one great (V.8.9). The later Athanasian Creed adopted this formulation.
  * *"Person":* Augustine was dissatisfied with the term. We say "three persons," "not in order to say that, but in order not to be silent" (*ne taceretur*, V.9.10; VII.4.7–6.11). He was uneasy with the Greek formula "one *ousia*, three *hypostaseis*," whose literal Latin rendering, "one essence, three substances," sounded tritheistic.
* **Inseparable operations:** The three persons act inseparably in every external work, such as creation (I.4.7). Distinctive missions—the Son's incarnation, the Spirit's sending—are appropriated to particular persons, though accomplished by all three.
* **The psychological analogies (Books VIII–XV):** Augustine develops a series of triads in the mind.
  * *Lover, beloved, and love* (VIII.10.14; IX.2.2).
  * *Mind, its knowledge of itself, and its love of itself* (*mens, notitia, amor*, Book IX). The three are equal and mutually contained.
  * *Memory of self, understanding of self, and will* (*memoria sui, intelligentia, voluntas*, X.11.17–12.19). These are not three lives but one life, not three minds but one mind, not three substances but one substance. Each is said relatively to the others, and each contains the others.
  * *The best image:* The image is fully realized only when the mind remembers, understands, and loves God (XIV.12.15). Then it is wise, and the image is restored.
* **Disanalogies (Book XV):** Augustine acknowledges important differences. In a human being, memory, understanding, and will belong to one person, whereas the divine persons are three. I *have* memory; I am not my memory. The analogy helps understanding, but it does not make the mystery transparent.
* **The Filioque:** The Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, but "principally" (*principaliter*) from the Father (XV.17.29; XV.26.47). Augustine's formulation became the basis of the Western *Filioque* doctrine, one of the issues in the later schism with Eastern Christianity.
* **Interpretive debate:** Théodore de Régnon's late nineteenth-century paradigm held that the Western tradition, following Augustine, starts from divine unity, while the East starts from the three persons. Twentieth-century theologians such as Colin Gunton accused Augustine of tending toward modalism, reducing the persons to aspects of one mind. Michel René Barnes and Lewis Ayres have argued that this paradigm misreads Augustine. His Trinitarian theology is pro-Nicene and emphasizes the irreducible distinction of persons alongside inseparable operation.
* **Major objections:** Can relations distinguish persons without introducing composition into a simple God? Aquinas's answer, "subsistent relations," was developed from Augustine. Psychological analogies risk assimilating divine persons to faculties of a single individual. The notion of "person" in Augustine differs markedly from Boethius' later definition: "an individual substance of a rational nature."
* **Philosophical significance:** *On the Trinity* shaped the logic of relations and the concept of person in medieval philosophy. It developed the most systematic ancient account of the structure of self-consciousness, since the psychological analogies required a detailed analysis of the mind's relations to itself. It also influenced the philosophical theology of Anselm, Aquinas, and Hegel, whose speculative Trinity reworks Augustinian themes.

**Philosophical Development and Internal Tensions**
* **Central problem:** Augustine's thought developed over more than forty years of writing, through several intellectual commitments and controversies. Unusually, he reviewed and corrected his own works in the *Retractations* (426–427). Whether his career shows fundamental breaks or a coherent development is one of the main interpretive questions about him.
* **Biography and phases:**
  * *Cicero's* Hortensius *(373):* At nineteen Augustine read this lost exhortation to philosophy and was converted to the love of wisdom (*Confessions* III.4.7).
  * *Manichaeism (c. 373–383):* As an auditor, he accepted a materialist dualism and a rationalist critique of the Old Testament. He was disillusioned by the inability of the Manichaean bishop Faustus to answer his questions (V.3.3–7.13).
  * *Academic skepticism (c. 383–384):* In Rome he inclined toward the Academics' suspension of judgment (V.10.19).
  * *Milan (384–386):* As professor of rhetoric he heard Ambrose's sermons, which used spiritual interpretation to answer Manichaean objections to Scripture. He read "the books of the Platonists" (VII.9.13) and had experiences of intellectual ascent (VII.10.16–17.23). His conversion in the garden followed (VIII.12.29).
  * *Cassiciacum and baptism (386–387):* He retreated to a villa with friends and wrote the early dialogues. He was baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387. He shared the vision at Ostia with his mother Monica (IX.10.23–26).
  * *Priest and bishop (391; 395/396):* In Hippo he engaged in controversies with Manichaeans, Donatists, Pelagians, and pagans until his death during the Vandal siege of Hippo in 430.
* **Platonism and conversion:** Prosper Alfaric argued in 1918 that Augustine's conversion of 386 was to Neoplatonism, not Christianity, and that the early dialogues are essentially Platonist. Pierre Courcelle showed that Milanese Christianity, notably Ambrose and Simplician, was itself deeply Platonic. The consensus now holds that Augustine converted to a Platonist Christianity. Christianity provided the authority and the way (Christ the mediator); Platonism provided the conceptual means of understanding incorporeal reality.
* **Freedom and grace:** Augustine's views on freedom and grace developed most sharply.
  * *Early works:* *On Free Choice* emphasizes free will to refute Manichaean determinism. Pelagians later quoted it against him (*Retractations* I.9).
  * *Ad Simplicianum (396):* This is the decisive turn to the priority of grace.
  * *The anti-Pelagian works:* These harden the doctrines of original sin, irresistible grace, and predestination.
  
  Kurt Flasch, in *Logik des Schreckens*, argued that the turn of 396 was a radical break, introducing a "logic of terror" into Western thought. Carol Harrison argued for continuity: the primacy of grace and the need for divine help were present from the early writings. Peter Brown's biography, especially the epilogue added to the new edition of 2000, emphasizes development and Augustine's increasingly somber view of human nature, without a single rupture.
* **Philosophy and the happy life:** The early dialogues are optimistic about the possibility of attaining wisdom and happiness in this life through philosophy and the liberal arts. The later Augustine rejected this as presumptuous. Happiness is reserved for the next life, and in this life we have only hope (*Retractations* I.2, on *On the Happy Life*; *City of God* XIX.4). He also retracted his praise of Plato and the Platonists as excessive (*Retractations* I.1.4).
* **Body and soul:** The early works' Platonist depreciation of the body yielded to an emphasis on the goodness of the body and the resurrection. In *City of God* XXII, Augustine affirms the full bodily reality of the resurrected person.
* **Unresolved questions:** Augustine left the following open:
  * The origin of the soul (creationism versus traducianism), which affects the transmission of original sin.
  * The relation of time as *distentio animi* to cosmic time.
  * The precise mechanism of illumination.
  * The fate of unbaptized infants, about which he expressed distress.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine's career is a case study in how commitments to authority, philosophical reflection, polemical controversy, and pastoral responsibility shape a philosopher's development. His *Retractations* is a rare self-critical review of an entire oeuvre. The tensions in his thought—between rational inquiry and authority, freedom and grace, Platonist ascent and Christian incarnation—became the fault lines of later Western philosophy and theology.

**Philosophical Legacy**: `Augustinianism`
* **Central thesis:** Augustine transformed the inheritance of ancient philosophy, especially Platonism, into the framework of Latin medieval thought. He relocated questions about knowledge, evil, time, the self, happiness, and politics within a theology of creation, sin, and grace. In doing so he generated problems that remained central to Western philosophy.
* **Debts to Platonism:**
  * *From Plato and Plotinus:* The immutability of true being, the hierarchy of existence, the intelligible world (relocated in God's mind), the inner ascent of the soul, evil as privation, and time as the life of soul. On evil there is a crucial difference: for Plotinus, evil is matter as absolute privation; for Augustine, matter is good and evil is a privation in a good nature, primarily in the will.
  * *From Porphyry:* Augustine engaged Porphyry's search for a "universal way of liberating the soul" (*City of God* X.32). He argued that the Platonists saw the goal but not the way: Christ the mediator (*Confessions* VII.20.26–21.27). He rejected theurgy and worship of intermediary spirits.
* **Medieval reception:**
  * *Boethius* extended Augustine's Trinitarian theology and his reflection on eternity and foreknowledge.
  * *Anselm* claimed in the preface to the *Monologion* that he said nothing inconsistent with Augustine's *On the Trinity*. His *Proslogion* begins from the Augustinian formula of faith seeking understanding. Its argument for God builds on Augustine's identification of God with "that than which nothing is better."
  * *Bonaventure and the Franciscan school* defended Augustinian illumination and the primacy of will and love against the Aristotelians.
  * *Aquinas* integrated Augustine with Aristotle. He naturalized illumination as the agent intellect's participation in divine light. He adopted the privation theory, the eternity of God, the doctrine of divine ideas, the definition of eternal law (*On Free Choice* I.6.15; *Against Faustus* XXII.27), and the conditions of just war.
* **Early modern reception:**
  * *Descartes* and the *cogito*: Arnauld pointed out the parallel in the Fourth Objections. Descartes acknowledged it but claimed a different use: Augustine used the argument against skepticism and for the soul's resemblance to the Trinity, whereas Descartes made it the foundation of a system. Stephen Menn argued that Descartes's metaphysics is substantially Augustinian.
  * *Luther*, an Augustinian friar, and *Calvin* adopted the anti-Pelagian doctrines of bondage of the will and predestination.
  * *Jansenism:* Cornelius Jansen's *Augustinus* (1640), Port-Royal, and Pascal revived Augustinian grace within Catholicism and were condemned.
  * *Malebranche* developed an ontologist reading of illumination: we see all things in God.
* **Modern and contemporary reception:**
  * *Phenomenology:* Husserl began his time lectures with *Confessions* XI. Heidegger's 1921 lectures "Augustine and Neoplatonism" interpreted *Confessions* X as a phenomenology of factical life and anxiety, foreshadowing *Being and Time*. Hannah Arendt's dissertation *Love and Saint Augustine* (1929) studied Augustinian love and the neighbor.
  * *Philosophy of language:* Wittgenstein began the *Philosophical Investigations* with Augustine's account of language learning.
  * *Hermeneutics:* Gadamer, Ricoeur, and others engaged *On Christian Doctrine* and *Confessions* XI.
  * *Political thought:* Political Augustinianism in the Middle Ages, Niebuhr's Christian realism, and contemporary debates about secularism and liberalism.
  * *Philosophy of religion:* The free-will defense (Plantinga), privation theory, divine simplicity, and divine timelessness remain central topics.
  * *Literature:* The *Confessions* created the genre of introspective autobiography, later developed by Rousseau and others.
* **How Augustine transformed ancient problems:**
  * *Evil:* From the cosmological problem of matter to the problem of the will's turning away from God.
  * *Knowledge:* From recollection or abstraction to illumination by a divine teacher present within.
  * *Time:* From the measure of cosmic motion to the soul's experience of memory, attention, and expectation.
  * *Self-knowledge:* From a Delphic precept to a first-person method grounded in the mind's certainty of itself.
  * *Happiness:* From a goal achievable through philosophical virtue to an eschatological gift of grace.
  * *Politics:* From the search for the best constitution to the critique of every political community's claim to justice.
  * *Moral psychology:* From the intellectualist account of action to the analysis of will, love, and habit.
* **Assessment:** Augustine's influence on Western philosophy and theology is difficult to overstate, but influence is not correctness. Several of his doctrines have been widely questioned: inherited guilt, predestination, the coercion of heretics, the acceptance of slavery, and the damnation of unbaptized infants. Other contributions remain live philosophical resources:
  * The anti-skeptical self-certainty argument.
  * The privation theory.
  * The analysis of time-consciousness.
  * The account of the divided will.
  * The theory of signs and the critique of ostension.
  * The ethics of ordered love.
  * Political realism about the limits of politics.
* **Philosophical significance:** Augustine is the principal bridge between ancient philosophy and medieval thought in the Latin West. His work defined the problems that later philosophers inherited, and he made the first-person perspective, the will, and the relation between finite minds and transcendent truth central to the philosophical tradition.

### Proclus ☆ (412–485 CE)

**Position:** Athenian Neoplatonism · Systematic Metaphysics · Platonic Theology · Philosophy of Mathematics

* **Name:** Proclus, also known as Proclus Lycius (Ancient Greek: Πρόκλος, *Próklos*).
* **Life:** Born in Constantinople; studied and taught in Athens, where he became head of the Neoplatonic school.
* **Contributions:** One of the most systematic philosophers of Late Antiquity. Developed an elaborate metaphysical hierarchy and a rigorous account of causation, participation, unity, procession, and reversion.
* **Major Works:** *Elements of Theology*, *Platonic Theology*, *Commentary on Plato's Timaeus*, *Commentary on Plato's Parmenides*, *Commentary on the First Book of Euclid's Elements*.

**The One and Metaphysical Hierarchy**
* The One, Henads, Divine Orders, Intelligible and Intellective Reality.
* How can the absolute unity of the first principle generate a differentiated structure of reality?
* *Elements of Theology*, *Platonic Theology*.

**Remaining, Procession, and Reversion**
* Remaining (*Monē*), Procession (*Prohodos*), Reversion (*Epistrophē*), Causal Dependence.
* Why must every effect remain related to its cause while becoming distinct from it? How can what proceeds from a principle return to that principle?
* *Elements of Theology*.

**Participation and Ontological Dependence**
* Participation (*Methexis*), Participated and Unparticipated Principles, Causal Series.
* How can a single universal principle be present in many distinct beings?

**Being, Life, and Intellect**
* Being, Life, Intellect, Ontological Triads.
* What explanatory role do triadic structures play in the organization of reality?
* *Elements of Theology*.

**The Soul and Philosophical Ascent**
* Soul, Contemplation, Purification, Reversion, Divine Knowledge.
* How does the soul return toward its intelligible causes?

**Mathematics and Philosophical Demonstration**
* Mathematical Objects, Hypotheses, Demonstration, Geometry.
* How are mathematical reasoning and metaphysical explanation related?
* *Commentary on the First Book of Euclid's Elements*.

**Platonic Exegesis and Theological Philosophy**
* Plato's *Parmenides*, *Timaeus*, Divine Hierarchy, Metaphysical Interpretation.
* Can Plato's dialogues be reconstructed as a unified theological-metaphysical system?
