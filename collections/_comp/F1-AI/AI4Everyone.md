## P6. What Machine Learning Can and Cannot Do

In this video and the next, I hope to help you develop intuition about what AI can and cannot do. Before committing to an AI project, I usually conduct technical diligence myself or ask engineers to do so. This means examining the data, the inputs and outputs A and B, and thinking through whether the project is feasible.

Unfortunately, some CEOs have inflated expectations and ask engineers to do things that the technology cannot do. I want to show examples of both capabilities and limitations so that you can choose more promising projects.

You have already seen applications such as spam filtering, speech recognition, and machine translation. One imperfect rule of thumb is that **pretty much anything you can do with a second of thought can probably be automated, now or soon, using supervised learning.** The idea is to learn an input-to-output mapping for a relatively simple task.

For example, you can identify the positions of other cars in less than a second, or look at a phone and quickly tell whether it is scratched. Recognizing and transcribing a short piece of speech also does not take many seconds of thought. This is an imperfect heuristic, but it gives you a way to start thinking about suitable tasks.

In contrast, accurately predicting a company's future stock price from only its historical price is something that AI cannot reliably do. A person cannot generally do this with a second of thought—or even with much longer—and we should not expect machine learning to solve it accurately either.

Suppose the input A is a stock's recent price history and the output B is its price one month later. A simple algorithm might fit a straight line to the data and extrapolate. But fitting the line over one time period could give the red line shown here, while fitting it over a shorter window could give the blue line. The predicted value changes dramatically depending on the implementation.

The underlying problem is that the historical price alone is not very predictive of the future price. Future prices contain substantial randomness, which makes accurate prediction difficult. This is why attempts to apply machine learning in this simple way have not been successful.

For completeness, traders sometimes find other useful inputs. For example, legally obtained web-traffic or foot-traffic data might help estimate a company's sales. Combining that information with price history could provide some predictive power. However, these inputs can be complex or costly to acquire, and they do not eliminate the stock market's inherent uncertainty.

Here are two further guidelines for thinking about feasibility. First, **learning a simple concept is more likely to be feasible.** There is no formal definition of “simple” here, but a judgment that takes less than a second, or only a small number of seconds, is suggestive. Spotting other cars through a windshield seems relatively simple. Finding clever signals that predict a company's sales is considerably less simple.

Second, a problem is more likely to be feasible when plenty of relevant data is available. For supervised learning, that means having both the input A and the desired output B. To detect scratches on phones, A would be phone images and B would be labels indicating whether each phone is scratched. Thousands of labeled images would improve your chances of building an accurate detector.

AI is the new electricity, and it is transforming industries, but it is **NOT** magic. *Look for feasible, valuable tasks rather than assuming the technology can solve every problem.* I hope these examples begin to sharpen your intuition. Let's continue with more examples in the next video.

## P14. How to Choose an AI Project, Part 1

If you want to try your hand at an AI project, how do you choose something worthwhile? Do not expect the right idea to appear overnight. Sometimes it does, but it can take days or weeks to identify an idea worth pursuing. Let's look at a framework for brainstorming projects.

Imagine one circle containing the things AI can do and another containing the things that would be valuable for your business. **The projects we want lie at the intersection: technically feasible and valuable to the business.**

AI experts tend to understand what belongs in the first circle. Domain experts—people who know your business, whether sales, marketing, agriculture, or another field—understand the second. I often bring both groups together to brainstorm. These cross-functional teams combine technical knowledge with business knowledge to identify opportunities that satisfy both requirements.

Here are three questions or principles I have found useful. First, when thinking about concrete projects, focus on **AUTOMATING TASKS**, rather than entire jobs. AI's effect on employment is an important societal issue, but a job is usually made up of many different tasks.

Consider a call center. Employees answer calls, respond to emails, and take actions such as issuing refunds. Among those tasks, routing a call or email to the right person may be particularly suitable for machine learning. Looking at the individual activities lets you select a practical project instead of trying to automate everything at once.

Radiology provides another example. Radiologists read X-rays, but they also continue their education, consult other doctors, mentor junior doctors, and sometimes speak directly with patients. Examining these tasks individually may lead you to choose assistance with reading X-rays as a promising project. That is much more specific than trying to automate the radiologist's whole job.

When you look at your business, identify one or a few tasks that machine learning might help with. This gives the technical and domain experts a concrete starting point.

Second, ask, “What are the main drivers of business value?” When I meet CEOs to brainstorm projects, I often ask this question. Finding AI or data science applications that strengthen those drivers can be valuable.

Third, ask, “What are the main pain points in the business?” Some can be addressed with AI and others cannot. Understanding them helps focus the discussion on problems that matter.

One final piece of advice is that you can make progress **EVEN WITHOUT BIG DATA**. More data is usually helpful, apart from the costs of storing and transmitting it, and I certainly like having plenty of data. In some businesses, data is an important source of competitive advantage.

Web search is one example. It has a long tail of rare queries, and observing what people click on for those queries helps a search engine improve its results. Large amounts of data can make the leading systems difficult to compete with.

Nevertheless, big data can be overhyped. Suppose you want to inspect coffee mugs automatically and detect defective ones. A million examples of good and bad mugs would provide a substantial training set, but I hope you have not had to manufacture a million defective mugs to obtain it. That would be an expensive amount of waste.

Sometimes you can begin with a hundred or a thousand examples, or even as few as ten. The amount needed is highly problem-dependent, and an AI engineer can help estimate it. Other problems do require much larger data sets, and a thousand images might not be enough for good performance.

*Do not abandon an idea simply because you do not yet have a large data set; first investigate what the particular problem requires.* The goal of brainstorming is to find projects that AI can accomplish and that matter to your business. ## P15. How to Choose an AI Project, Part 2

Perhaps you now have several ideas for AI projects. Before committing to one, how do you determine whether it is worthwhile? If it will take only a few days, you might simply try it. But a project that requires months of work deserves a more careful assessment.

Before a large project, I usually conduct due diligence. The term has a specific legal meaning, but here I use it informally to mean spending time checking that what we hope is true really is true. We want projects that are both feasible and valuable. Technical diligence assesses feasibility, while business diligence assesses value.

**Technical diligence asks whether the proposed system can actually achieve the required performance.** If you want speech recognition with 95% accuracy, consult AI experts or examine relevant technical literature to determine whether that is plausible. If a coffee-mug inspection system needs 99% accuracy, ask whether the available technology can achieve that on your problem.

A second question is how much data will be needed and whether you can acquire it. Third, estimate the engineering timeline: how long the project will take and how many people it will require. These questions help turn a promising idea into a realistic plan.

Business diligence asks whether the system would actually create value. Many projects reduce costs by automating tasks or making operations more efficient. Others increase revenue, for example by helping more customers complete their purchases. A project might also enable a new product or line of business.

The question is whether the system at its achievable performance level supports the business goal. Would a speech system with 95% accuracy, or an inspection system with 99.9% accuracy, deliver the intended benefit? That needs to be examined for the actual application, whether you are improving an existing operation or building something new.

I often use spreadsheet financial models to estimate the value: how much money might be saved, what increase in revenue is a reasonable assumption, and how the economics work overall. For a project requiring months of effort, this analysis is worth doing before committing.

A third kind of diligence, though not explicitly listed on the slide, is ethical diligence. AI can perform tasks that make money without making society better off. **Technical feasibility and commercial value are NOT sufficient reasons to build something.** I hope you also consider whether the project benefits people and society. We will return to these issues in the final week.

Another planning question is whether to build or buy. This is familiar in IT: most companies buy computers and Wi-Fi routers rather than making their own. The same choice appears in AI.

Machine learning projects can be done in-house or outsourced, and I have seen both work well. Outsourcing may provide quicker access to talent and help a project start sooner. Eventually, it can be valuable to build an internal AI team; we will discuss that in the transformation playbook.

Data science projects are more commonly done in-house. They can be outsourced, but the work often depends on deep, day-to-day knowledge of the business. That close connection makes an internal team particularly useful. As a proportion of projects, I see more data science work kept inside the company than machine learning work.

In any industry, some capabilities become standard. A common guideline is to build what is specialized to your business or creates a distinctive, defensible advantage, and buy what other organizations can provide as a standard solution.

One of my teams expressed this as, “Don't sprint in front of a train.” Imagine an engineer trying to run faster and faster ahead of a train. You might stay ahead briefly, but maintaining a competing implementation can become increasingly difficult as that broader effort catches up.

When a strong standard solution is emerging, it may be more effective to adopt it or build on someone else's platform. We all have limited time, data, and engineering resources. *Focus those resources on projects that are distinctive and can make the largest difference to your organization.*

## P16. Working with an AI Team

How can you work with an AI team to carry out a promising project? If you do not yet have access to a team, you or some engineering colleagues can often learn enough from one or two machine learning or deep learning courses to make a reasonable start.

When working with a team, it helps to specify **clear acceptance criteria**. I have worked extensively on automated visual inspection, so let's use that example. Suppose the goal is to detect defects in coffee mugs with at least 95% accuracy. That gives the team a target, but we also need to say how accuracy will be measured.

The team needs a data set of mug images with the desired outputs: labels saying which mugs are acceptable and which are defective. As part of the acceptance criteria, make sure there is a data set on which to evaluate performance. Otherwise, the team cannot tell whether it has achieved the 95% target.

This evaluation data is called a test set. It may not need to be enormous; perhaps a thousand images would be sufficient for this example. An AI expert can help determine how large it should be to assess performance reliably.

One characteristic of AI projects is that performance is often specified statistically. Instead of requiring a system to perform perfectly every time, we may require a certain percentage of correct answers. When setting acceptance criteria, consider whether the requirement should describe average performance or the proportion of cases handled correctly.

Let's examine how teams use data. Two important data sets are the training set and the test set. The training set contains examples of inputs A—images of mugs—and desired outputs B—labels indicating whether the mugs are acceptable or defective.

A machine learning algorithm uses these examples to learn an A-to-B mapping. The result is software that can receive an image and predict the appropriate label. In other words, the training set is the information from which the system learns how to perform the task.

The test set contains a different collection of labeled images. To evaluate the system, the team gives it those images, checks its predictions, and compares them with the known labels. **The test set is separate from the training set.**

Suppose there are three test images: two acceptable mugs and one defective mug. If the system labels all three as acceptable, it gets two out of three correct, giving 66.7% accuracy. The slide uses only three images to illustrate the idea; actual training and test sets would be much larger. For many problems, the training set is considerably larger than the test set.

You may also hear a team ask for a development set, or “dev” or validation set. This is an additional, separate set of examples used during development. The technical reasons for maintaining these separate data sets are beyond this course, but a request for both validation data and test data is reasonable.

A pitfall to avoid is expecting **100% ACCURACY** from the software. There are several reasons this may be unattainable. First, machine learning has limitations, and the task may be difficult for the available technology. Second, the training set may not contain enough examples for the system to learn accurately.

Third, data can be messy or mislabeled. In the slide, a green mug looks acceptable to me, but its label says it is defective. An incorrect label can hurt the system's measured performance. Other examples may be genuinely ambiguous: a very small scratch might be acceptable to one inspector and a defect to another.

These problems can sometimes be reduced. You can collect more data, correct mislabeled examples, or ask factory experts to agree on how to label borderline cases. But valuable AI systems do **NOT** always need perfect accuracy.

*Agree on a performance target that is technically achievable and sufficient for the business need.* Discuss the target with your engineers and use technical and business diligence to determine what is reasonable.

## P20. Case Study: Self-Driving Cars

## P24. AI Pitfalls to Avoid

I hope you use AI to build valuable projects for yourself or your company and improve life for others as well. Along the way, there are several common pitfalls worth avoiding. Let's go through five pairs of mistakes and better approaches.

First, do **NOT** expect AI to solve everything. Be realistic about the limits of the technology, data, and engineering resources. Technical diligence and business diligence help identify projects that are both feasible and valuable.

Second, do **NOT** hire two or three machine learning engineers and expect them alone to discover the best use cases for the company. Pair engineering talent with business expertise. Cross-functional work helps identify problems that matter and solutions that can be built.

Third, do **NOT** expect the project to work on the first attempt. **AI development is usually iterative.** Plan for several attempts and improvements before reaching a satisfactory result.

Fourth, do **NOT** assume traditional planning processes apply unchanged. Work with the AI team to establish appropriate timelines, milestones, key performance indicators, and other metrics. The uncertainty and development process can differ from those of non-AI projects.

Fifth, do **NOT** wait for superstar AI engineers before doing anything. Keep building the team, but start with the people you have. Many engineers, including those who learned through online courses, can build valuable applications.

## P30. Discrimination and Bias

In practice, a representation may contain hundreds or thousands of numbers, but two dimensions let us draw it on a chart.

If the patterns in internet text place “homemaker” at that position, the model produces the biased answer. This simplified example shows how statistical relationships in the data can become stereotypes in the system's output.

## P32. Adverse Uses of AI

AI is incredibly powerful and the vast majority of users of AI are making people, companies, countries, society better off. But there are a few adverse uses of AI as well. Let's take a look at some of them and discuss what we can do about them.

## P33. AI and Developing Economies

AI-driven automation can affect the economic opportunities available to developing countries. If automation removes some of the lower rungs of the economic ladder, we need to consider whether AI can also provide a trampoline that helps countries reach higher rungs more quickly.

Earlier waves of technology show that developing economies can sometimes leapfrog established systems. In the United States, many people already had landline telephones connected by wires. That installed infrastructure meant the transition to mobile phones took time.

In contrast, many developing economies, including India and China, built fewer landlines and moved more directly to mobile phones. They did not need to install physical telephone cables in every home before adopting a more advanced technology.

We see a similar pattern in payments. Countries with established credit-card systems can be slower to adopt mobile payments than countries without such entrenched systems. Online education offers another example. Where there are not yet enough physical schools and universities, leaders may look for ways to expand education more quickly through digital resources.

Developed economies are also adopting these technologies, but the absence of an established system can sometimes let developing economies move faster. *A lack of older infrastructure can, in some circumstances, become an opportunity to adopt a newer approach directly.*

In the landscape discussed in this course, the United States has a lead, particularly in generative AI, with China close behind. The United Kingdom, Canada, India, Korea, countries in Latin America, and many others also have active AI communities. AI is still developing, and I believe every country has opportunities ahead.

My advice is to use AI to strengthen a country's existing or emerging industry specialties. Most countries do **NOT** need to build another general-purpose web search engine; strong services already exist, and that was a major competition of the previous decade.

If a country has a strong coffee-production industry, it may be particularly well positioned to develop AI for that industry. Doing so can strengthen capabilities it already possesses. Rather than competing with the United States or China in every aspect of AI, focus on what the country does well and what it wants to develop.

Public-private partnerships can accelerate progress. Governments and companies can work together in regulated sectors such as healthcare, transportation, and finance. Thoughtful rules can protect citizens while allowing beneficial applications to develop, supporting both local economic growth and technological progress.

Developing economies should also invest in education. Because the field is still evolving, there is room to build an AI workforce and participate meaningfully in this new economy.

**Leadership matters during technological change.** In the United States, we once trusted the government to put a person on the Moon, and it succeeded. AI similarly creates opportunities for leadership in government, business, and education. Those leaders can help their countries adopt the technology in ways that improve citizens' lives and benefit people elsewhere too.

## P34. AI and Jobs

AI is automation on steroids. Automation affected employment long before modern AI, but AI greatly expands the set of tasks that can be automated. That accelerates its impact on jobs.

How many jobs will disappear, and how many will be created? I do not think anyone has a firm answer. Let's examine some studies to understand the possibilities discussed in this course.

The McKinsey Global Institute estimate cited here projects that automation could displace 400–800 million jobs by 2030. These are large numbers. On the other hand, the report also discusses the possibility of even greater job creation. Other studies give different estimates; the Goldman Sachs study cited here gives a lower figure of 300 million jobs by 2033.

There is substantial uncertainty, but broad agreement that the effects could be significant. I find it encouraging that some studies anticipate more jobs being created than displaced, even though retraining could be a major challenge in some sectors.

We may not yet have names for many future jobs. Possibilities include drone-traffic optimizers, designers of 3D-printed clothing, or specialists in personalized DNA-based drug design. Alongside concern about displacement, there is reason to expect new kinds of work.

How do researchers estimate the potential for automation? One common method is to break a job into tasks. For a radiologist or taxi driver, identify the activities involved and estimate how suitable each is for AI automation. If most tasks are highly automatable, the job may face greater displacement risk.

**AI is applied to tasks, and analyzing those tasks helps us estimate its effect on jobs.** This is usually more useful than treating an occupation as one indivisible activity.

The chart shown here, adapted from McKinsey data, lists ten occupation groups with high automation potential in the study's 2033 scenario. Figures such as 87 or 82 beside the bars represent estimated percentages of work tasks that could be automated. The groups include office work, mechanical installation and repair, business and legal work, customer service, and sales.

Generative AI changes which tasks are exposed. Tools that write natural-sounding text, compose empathetic emails, and converse with people make more writing and communication work amenable to automation. Comparing estimates with and without these tools shows increases in areas such as office support and legal work.

The other column shows the share of the global workforce in each occupation group. Together, the ten groups shown account for more than 70% of workers. This is why helping people navigate the transition matters.

One possible response is conditional basic income. Universal basic income provides payments without conditions. I believe people deserve a safety net, and for unemployed people who are able to learn, I think support that also encourages continued learning may be especially useful.

Providing a structure for education and development can improve the chances of reentering the workforce. That benefits individuals, their families, society, and the tax base supporting the program.

A second response is to build a lifelong-learning society. By taking this course, you are already participating in that idea. The old model of studying for four years and relying on that education for the next forty is **NO LONGER** sufficient in a rapidly changing world.

Governments, companies, and individuals can all support continued learning. As some jobs disappear and others emerge, people who keep developing their skills are better positioned to adapt. I believe learning should continue throughout life, including after college.

Political responses are also being explored, from supporting job creation to legislation promoting fair treatment. I hope societies find effective ways to manage these changes.

People sometimes ask what they should do if they want to work in AI. A radiology resident early in his career once asked me whether he should leave radiology and learn AI instead. That is possible: people can retrain from scratch, and many have done so.

But I suggested another option: work at the intersection of **AI AND RADIOLOGY**. With his existing medical knowledge and additional AI skills, he could be better positioned than most people to contribute in that area.

*Combine AI with what you already know to create a distinctive contribution.* You can learn the technology through courses and other resources while applying your existing expertise to valuable problems.
